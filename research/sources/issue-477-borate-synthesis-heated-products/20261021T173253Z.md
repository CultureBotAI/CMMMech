## Mcallisterite 2014: the recovered graphics sharpen the unresolved questions

Figure 1 is now directly inspectable. Its flow separates residual MgO by filtration, takes the magnesium-borate/boric-acid aqueous stream through a 40 °C incubator, washes the solid with pure ethanol, and dries again at 40 °C. The twelve numbered streams distinguish solid MgO, boric acid, magnesium borate, water and ethanol, and end with a stream labelled pure magnesium borate. That process label is not a measured phase-purity fraction. Neither the flow diagram nor the complete methods supplies a recoverable reagent mass/solution-volume/wash-volume ledger or the full reaction inventory needed for a yield calculation.

The authors refer to their admontite study (DOI [10.1007/s11164-013-1237-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11164-013-1237-6)) for analytical and yield methods. Current scoped author-posted cached text of that separate study supplies an MgO:H3BO3 ratio of 1:6 and defines yield as product moles divided by consumed key-reactant moles; it then identifies MgO as the key reactant and five parallel experiments. That source concerns admontite at different reaction conditions. It is not a full acquired PDF or a justified transfer of its exact feed quantities, five replicates, or normalization to the 2014 mcallisterite experiment. The 2014 paper reports four parallel experiments and an 85.80 ± 0.61% yield. Under a literal full two-Mg mcallisterite formula, unnormalized product moles per consumed MgO cannot exceed 0.5 mol/mol. A half-formula amount or stoichiometric normalization could alter that interpretation, but the necessary convention is not established. The reported yield remains a reported endpoint, not a validated boron or magnesium recovery fraction.

## Tunellite: keep three experimental histories separate

The 1972 main paper concerns selected Göcenoluk crystals. Its composition lists SrO/B2O3/H2O as 26.76/53.25/19.98%, summing to 99.99%; other displayed comparison compositions sum to 99.97 and 100.12%. These sums are diagnostics of the printed rounded entries and are not normalized assays.

The rate discrepancy is explicit. The methods specify TG at 60 °C/hour and DTA at 10 °C/minute, whereas Figure 15 labels both curves at 300 °C/hour. These correspond to 1, 10 and 5 °C/minute respectively. The original does not identify which should replace the others. Static heating, separate isothermal treatment, and dynamic scans also differ in sample mass and duration. XRD patterns were obtained after cooling, including a 24-hour interval, so they are not in situ equilibrium phase observations.

The later figures show why the historical product names must remain qualified. Figure 26 at 725 °C labels veatchite/p-veatchite; Figure 27 at 800 °C retains tunellite and veatchite annotations, and the text describes resemblance and incomplete final form. The historical oxide-hydrate expressions are not an automatic identity match to the modern accepted veatchite formula. Current tunellite has B/Sr = 6, whereas current veatchite's formula has B/Sr = 11/2. Pure water loss alone cannot change that ratio without another product, loss or a different identity assignment. Four water equivalents in the oxide representation also do not mean four molecular waters: current tunellite has three molecular waters and two hydroxyl groups. None of these diagnostics identifies a corrected phase assemblage.

The 1972 companion is a different experiment: a 5 g stoichiometric tunellite–Na2CO3 mixture was heated for three hours at listed temperatures, then extracted with hot water. The 300 °C/hour DTA comparison uses the mixture against tunellite; it cannot settle the unmixed paper's rate conflict. The authors convert a B2O3 assay to an anhydrous sodium-tetraborate expression using a printed factor of 1.66. The ideal formula-mass ratio for Na2B4O7/(2 B2O3), under the audit's stated mass convention, is approximately 1.4452. No hydration convention or percentage denominator is selected to repair the discrepancy. The published percentage curves, approximate yield statements and mixed cooled patterns do not establish total-boron recovery or a pure isolated borax product.

The newly acquired 2012 paper uses a different Kırka specimen, ground below 250 mesh and calcined for five hours at 400, 710 or 800 °C before cooling in a desiccator. Its non-isothermal scans run at 10, 15 and 20 °C/minute under nitrogen, with a stated 150 mL/minute gas flow. These conditions cannot be transferred to the 1972 crystals. Figure 1's two annotated losses total 17.94%; the text's dehydration/dehydroxylation stage descriptions are retained rather than harmonized into a new pathway.

Figure 2's 710 and 800 °C patterns carry both strontium-borate and veatchite symbols. The prose also reuses PDF card `01-074-2316` for differently named products. Neither the plot nor the captions supply quantitative phase fractions. Reported BET areas are 2.41 m²/g initially, 2.62 at 400 °C and 0.38 at 800 °C: the first step increases, so the blanket decrease statement is not monotonic across all displayed values. Table 1's 21-model comparisons give F3 the highest listed R² at each rate and a mean Ea of about 144.38 kJ/mol. A best empirical correlation is not proof of microscopic reaction order; Table 2's literal model formulas were inspected without corrective refitting. These observations are useful separate context, not a pure-product claim or an authoritative repair of the 1972 study.
