Metastudtite mineral

Metastudtite is the uranyl peroxide dihydrate mineral UO4·2H2O, equivalent in composition to (UO2)(O2)(H2O)2. The September 2026 IMA list gives approved status A and approval accession 1981-055. It is distinct from studtite, other uranium hydrates, elemental uranium and uranium commodities. The original description concerns natural Shinkolobwe material and a separate heated natural-studtite mixture; modern thermal and aqueous experiments used synthetic substrates. A particular historical structure model is not asserted as the unique current structure. No criticality, microbial activity, isolated uranium recovery or industrial deployment is inferred.

Microbial & Biochemical Mechanisms (3)

Abiotic production in a heated natural studtite mixture

other

Heating natural studtite-bearing fibers produced a powder pattern dominated by metastudtite together with curite. The experiment supports a hydrate transformation in a mixed natural specimen; it does not establish a phase-pure product or a quantitative yield.

Experimental Context & Analytical Limits:
Deliens and Piret heated yellow studtite fibers with a reddish core at 100 degrees C for two hours. Their separately analyzed natural metastudtite fibers also contained uranium-lead hydrate material in the cores. Natural morphology alone was unreliable: historical collection fibers labeled studtite included studtite, metastudtite and lepersonnite.
Curated Evidence Points:
Support Source: deliens1983
Page 457 and Table 1 identify metastudtite plus curite after the two-hour treatment. Table 1 distinguishes the authors' heated natural-studtite pattern from natural metastudtite, a prior synthetic preparation and an earlier heated specimen. These columns are not replicate measurements of one substrate.
Partial Source: deliens1983
Natural fibers gave additional reflections attributed to uranium-lead hydrates; neither a pure precursor nor full conversion was established. The historical irreversibility statement cites earlier work and is not a controlled reversibility test in this experiment. The paper's printed calculated-density unit is inconsistent with an ordinary mineral density and is not adopted.

Abiotic decomposition of metastudtite formed during studtite heating

other

Metastudtite formed in situ during heating of synthetic studtite subsequently lost crystalline diffraction features and developed amorphous uranium-bearing products. Dehydration and peroxide loss are supported interpretations; a single pure final oxide and a unique molecular pathway were not established.

Experimental Context & Analytical Limits:
Spano et al. synthesized the starting studtite from equal volumes of 0.5 M uranyl nitrate and 30 percent H2O2, washed and air-dried it, and heated samples from approximately 25 to 250 degrees C. PXRD and Raman measurements were in air every 5 degrees C; TGA used approximately 25 mg in continuous argon after a 30-minute equilibration at 25 degrees C. Nominal rates of 1, 2, 5 and 10 degrees C/min differed from effective PXRD rates 0.075-0.080 and Raman rates 0.830-3.283 degrees C/min because of measurement dwell times. Metastudtite was an intermediate of these experiments, not an independently identical pure starting powder for every thermal arm.
Curated Evidence Points:
Support Source: spano2020
Proof pages E-I and Figures 3, 7-11 document loss of metastudtite diffraction and changes in uranyl/peroxo Raman bands. PXRD product-dominant crossovers averaged 144.0 +/- 1.8 degrees C under the long-dwell protocol. Raman model-derived crossovers ranged from 195.7 to 206.2 degrees C across the nominal rates. These method-dependent crossovers are not onset temperatures or a universal stability boundary. Table 3's second-order kinetic model is a fit to TGA conversion data, not independent proof of one elementary chemical reaction.
Partial Source: spano2020
Pages G and I and Figures 8 and 11 retain residual peroxide spectral contributions and differing mass losses at 250 degrees C. No run reached the theoretical 21.4 percent loss expected for full conversion of the starting studtite to U2O7. The authors explicitly leave final amorphous-product speciation unresolved. Starting-material water retention, probe depth, sample amount, heating schedule and air-versus-argon conditions limit direct comparison of techniques. The paper's Pnma metastudtite model is historical evidence, not a newly verified unique structure for all natural and synthetic metastudtite.

Abiotic early metastudtite formation on peroxide-exposed U3O8

other

Metastudtite was the largest identified alteration-phase contribution after one day on synthetic U3O8 exposed to peroxide, followed by studtite-dominated alteration spectra from day eight onward. Dissolution/reprecipitation was proposed; direct reversible solid-state hydration of an isolated metastudtite substrate was not tested.

Experimental Context & Analytical Limits:
Chamberlain et al. used separate approximately 50 mg alpha- and beta-U3O8 powders in 50 mL solutions with measured initial H2O2 concentrations 0.16, 0.0030 or 0.000054 M. Water-only U3O8 and peroxide-only controls were included. Beakers prepared in ambient atmosphere were parafilm-sealed between sampling; pH strips gave approximately 6.5 and no numerical incubation temperature was specified. Solid aliquots were air-dried for at least four hours before analysis. Fifteen Raman spots per aliquot represented spatial subsampling rather than independent reaction replicates.
Curated Evidence Points:
Support Source: chamberlain2025
Figure 4 and Tables S-2-S-4 and S-6-S-8 support the surface-phase succession in peroxide-added arms, with unaltered U3O8 often remaining the largest total component. Metastudtite's fitted contribution was reduced rather than uniformly exactly zero after day eight. For alpha-U3O8 in 0.16 M H2O2, Table S-2 gives 0.49734 on day 1 and 0.0225 on day 8; these normalized PLSR contributions are not bulk mass fractions or recovery yields.
Partial Source: chamberlain2025
Pages 10-11 explicitly limit conclusions to surface alteration. Small nonzero alteration components occur in fitted water-control spectra, without obvious SEM morphological change. Continuing U3O8 reaction, air drying before analysis and reference-spectrum selection prevent assigning the later studtite exclusively to dissolution of metastudtite or proving solid-state rehydration. The authors call for mechanism studies. Their observations limit an unconditional irreversibility claim; they do not establish reversible equilibrium, bulk conversion rates or a microbial process.
