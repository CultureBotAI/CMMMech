Alburnite is the natural silver-germanium sulfide-telluride mineral Ag8GeTe2S4, listed as approved IMA 2012-073 in the September 2026 IMA-CNMNC list. Baddeleyite

Baddeleyite is the natural monoclinic zirconium oxide mineral species ZrO2, listed as grandfathered in the September 2026 IMA-CNMNC list. It is distinct from elemental zirconium, zircon (ZrSiO4), synthetic zirconia products and other ZrO2 polymorphs. Huraiová, Hurai and Konečný (2011) characterized baddeleyite associated with a zoned zircon crystal separated from redeposited sediments of the Hajnáčka-Kostná dolina basaltic maar, southern Slovakia. Cathodoluminescence and backscattered-electron images show baddeleyite in melt pockets and in a reaction rim intergrown with sanidine and anorthoclase. Their Table 2 reports six representative baddeleyite microprobe analyses containing 96.65-98.27 wt.% ZrO2 and 0.99-1.06 wt.% HfO2, alongside analyses of the distinct host zircon. These oxide-equivalent specimen compositions are not elemental-metal percentages, mineral-wide purity or resource grades. The study uses microscopy and electron microprobe chemistry; it does not report a new diffraction refinement. Formation by reaction of zircon with Si-poor, Al-rich melt is the authors' geological interpretation, not a controlled transformation experiment. The reported absence of light rare earth elements is bounded by analytical detection limits, not proof of zero concentration. This identity and natural-occurrence evidence does not establish microbial extraction, recovery, industrial deployment or a dated criticality designation for the mineral.

Brookite

Brookite is the TiO2 mineral species listed by IMA-CNMNC in September 2026 with grandfathered status G and original-description year 1825. That year is not an IMA approval identifier. It is distinct from the rutile and anatase mineral species, from elemental titanium and from unspecified manufactured TiO2 particles. Liu and colleagues studied natural brookite from Magnet Cove, Arkansas, USA, obtained as specimen 8115 from the University of California, Berkeley Earth and Planetary Science collection. Selected black crystals were cleaned with 1 mol/L nitric acid, washed, dried, crushed and ground. Ambient powder XRD and Rietveld fitting support the brookite phase assignment; the subsequent high-pressure experiment is not a microbial assay. Two EDS locations on one ground particle yielded mean reported abundances of O 38.64, Ti 59.23 and Nb 2.13 weight percent. These are localized analyses, not a bulk ore grade, a representative mine inventory or evidence of a chemically pure endmember. Figure S2 compares powder before and after pressing near 8 MPa and supports no apparent phase change during that preparation step. Later compression in a diamond-anvil cell produces pressure- and medium-dependent TiO2 phase mixtures; the source explicitly treats the phase-content diagrams as kinetic, non-equilibrium observations. Its baddeleyite terminology refers to a TiO2 structural polymorph, not formation of the zirconium mineral species ZrO2. No microbial mechanism, titanium extraction yield, industrial recovery or constituent-derived criticality is asserted by this identity and natural-specimen record.

Cadmoindite

Cadmoindite is the cadmium-indium sulfide mineral species CdIn2S4, approved as IMA 2003-042 in the September 2026 IMA-CNMNC list. Chaplygin and colleagues (2004) described natural octahedral crystals from fumarolic crusts at Kudriavy volcano on Iturup Island in the Kuril Islands. Microscopy, fifteen electron-microprobe analyses and powder X-ray diffraction support a cubic thiospinel assignment, compared separately with synthetic CdIn2S4 and the iron analogue indite. The analyzed natural grains contain Fe and Zn and smaller Ge and Se contributions; ideal end-member stoichiometry is not a claim of specimen purity. Crystals occur in both pyrite-associated and pyrite-free assemblages, with cadmium-bearing wurtzite and other phases. The paper reports sampling roughly 15-30 centimetres below the crust surface at outgoing-gas temperatures of 450-600 degrees Celsius. Those are field sampling conditions, not directly observed crystallization temperatures or biological incubation conditions. Formation from cooling gas, including the proposed breakdown of metal chlorides, is inferred from the occurrence and cited gas modelling; it was not demonstrated by a controlled transformation experiment on this mineral. Separate laboratory synthesis conditions and electronic uses of synthetic CdIn2S4 do not establish recovery or deployment of natural cadmoindite. This species is distinct from elemental cadmium or indium, indite, bulk fumarolic crust and manufactured CdIn2S4 considered only by formula. The specimen study does not establish a bulk resource grade or extraction yield. No microbial mechanism or constituent-derived criticality designation is assigned.

Carnotite

Carnotite is the potassium uranyl vanadate mineral K2(UO2)2(VO4)2·3H2O. The September 2026 IMA-CNMNC list recognizes it with grandfathered status G; 1899 is the original-description year, not an IMA approval accession. This mineral-species owner is distinct from the broader carnotite group, elemental uranium or vanadium, mixed uranium ore and dissolved uranyl or vanadate salts. Glasauer et al. (2022) identified K-carnotite together with metatyuyamunite by X-ray diffraction in the final fine fraction of a natural uranium-vanadium sandstone incubated with living Shewanella putrefaciens CN32. The starting assemblage was identified as metatyuyamunite and fritzscheite, rather than a pure carnotite feed. Figure 1b and sections 3.1 and 3.3 support this bounded phase-occurrence comparison. Increased K and decreased Ca in the final fine fraction are consistent with interlayer-cation exchange. The study does not isolate a uniquely biological cause of carnotite formation: the authors recognize both biotic and abiotic pathways, and do not provide a matched final abiotic diffraction series. Section 3.2 describes tyuyamunite-to-carnotite conversion whereas the conclusion names fritzscheite as the precursor; this record assigns neither unique precursor. The shared laboratory experiment used 1.6 g of mixed sandstone in 80 mL defined medium with 10 mM sodium lactate and 1 mM phosphate, nitrogen degassing and autoclaving before inoculation, followed by an anoxic glovebox incubation extending to ten months. Treatments were in triplicate. These three mineral records refer to the same experiment, not independent replications. Bacteria-free solution controls were included, but do not isolate each phase's fate. The mixed-ore spectra support partial V reduction while U remained predominantly U(VI); results from the separate soluble-salt arm must not be transferred to this mineral. Dissolved U was low, rather than established to be exactly zero. Section 3.2 gives a V plateau time of 120 days while Figure 2 labels the time axes in hours, so no exact kinetic endpoint is adopted. The species identity and culture-associated phase occurrence are curated here without a mineral-specific mechanism assertion. Carnotite formation is not established as obligatory, exclusively biological, a pure recovered product or an industrial process. No criticality designation is inherited from its constituent elements.

This is the same heterogeneous block already described in the elpidite record, not independent sampling or a pure catapleiite feed. The paper does not provide sample-specific diffraction confirmation. The detailed analytical appendix and Supplementary Table 1 remain unavailable. Figure 3C labels material SZR while its caption calls it catapleiite; Table 4 and Table 6 repeat IL-356 under different material headings. Main-text and Table 5 sodium ranges also disagree. Their silver-exchange experiment used that thermally transformed derivative, whose framework differs from hydrated catapleiite. Its silver-bearing product is not a demonstrated exchange product of the unheated mineral.
