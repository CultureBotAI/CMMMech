Hazenite mineral

Hazenite is the hydrated potassium sodium magnesium phosphate mineral species KNaMg2(PO4)2(H2O)14, approved as IMA2007-061 and listed as approved in the September 2026 IMA list. It is distinct from elemental phosphorus, magnesium or potassium, the ammonium analog of hazenite, and mixed lake deposits or recovered fertilizers. The original description establishes a natural mineral associated with dried or decomposed cyanobacterial material; it does not establish exclusive biological formation, a quantified recovery process or industrial deployment. Constituent criticality is not inherited.

Microbial & Biochemical Mechanisms (1)

Hazenite associated with cyanobacterial remains at Mono Lake

biomineralization Microbial

Natural hazenite crystals occurred in or on completely dried or decomposed cyanobacterial material on calcium-carbonate tufa. Decomposition supplying phosphate and cell surfaces providing nucleation sites were proposed explanations. The accepted evidence supports mineral identity and spatial association with microbial remains, not a demonstrated causal requirement for metabolism or an exclusive biological pathway.

Experimental Context & Analytical Limits:
The studied material came from the south shore of Mono Lake, California, at 37 degrees 56 minutes 31 seconds N, 119 degrees 1 minute 42 seconds W, on porous substrates described as mainly calcite and aragonite. This is an observational field collection followed by laboratory mineral characterization, not a controlled culture trial. The original description calls the substrate organisms green algae (cyanobacteria) but supplies no taxonomic identification or strain accession for the field remains; the Lyngbya sp. culture cited from earlier work is not assigned to these specimens. The source describes the lake generally as alkaline and hypersaline from prior literature; those values are not specimen-specific measurements or a validated formation window. Time of crystallization, local pore-water pH, biomass state during nucleation and seasonal dependence were not established.
Curated Evidence Points:
Partial Source: yang_2011
Occurrence and Methods on page 676 and Figure 1 show or describe tufts of crystals associated with dried/decomposed cyanobacterial material. Twenty-point electron-microprobe averages, Raman spectroscopy and powder/single-crystal XRD (Tables 1-6 and Figures 2-3, pages 676-679) establish the reported mineral and its structure. Water in the empirical chemical analysis was calculated by difference to 100%, not independently measured by that analysis. Mineral identity and spatial association do not demonstrate that microorganisms caused crystallization.
Partial Source: yang_2011
Formation on pages 679-680 proposes phosphate release during breakdown of phosphorus-bearing organic matter and heterogeneous nucleation on cell membranes, with dissolved lake components supplying Na, K and Mg. No matched sterile substrate experiment, killed-versus-live comparison, phosphate-source tracer, kinetic series or quantitative recovery mass balance tests those proposals in the field material. The abstract and structural discussion suggest an exclusive biological role, which is not established by crystal structure or association and is not adopted here. The authors themselves leave biological control versus biological induction and seasonal effects unresolved.
Context_only Source: yang_2011
Page 679 recounts an earlier Lyngbya sp. culture discovery cited to Yang and Sun 2004: a 1:1 mixture of lake and distilled water solidified with 1.2% agar dried after growth, with crystals near dead colonies. That earlier original was not acquired in this curation, so this retrospective paragraph is not treated as an independently inspected culture experiment and its missing controls, incubation temperature, duration and strain identity are not filled in. Struvite experiments cited on page 680 are distinct studies and do not supply matched controls or causal proof for hazenite.
