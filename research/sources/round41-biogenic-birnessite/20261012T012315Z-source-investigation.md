## Accepted-source assay contract and original figure checks

**Formation stage (sections 2.1 and 3.1, pages 605–607).** Paraconiothyrium sp. WL-2, originating from a constructed wetland at Kaminokuni, Hokkaido, was cultured in 150–200 ml PY medium in 500 ml flasks with 150 mg/L initial Mn(II), initial pH 6.6, PIPES buffer, 0.5 mg wet inoculum as printed, 25 ± 2 °C, 100 rpm, light shielding and up to two weeks. Per litre, PY contains MgSO4·7H2O 0.6 g, CaCl2·2H2O 0.07 g, peptone and yeast extract 0.05 g each, and PIPES 4.53 g. The source describes collecting and rinsing precipitate on a 5 µm filter, then calls the freeze-dried material “filtrate”; this preparation wording remains ambiguous. Powder was freeze-dried 24 hours and stored at 4 °C. Formation is not supported here by an independently displayed sterile time course or enzyme intervention.

**Phase and composition.** Figure 1 has weak broad diffraction and the authors explicitly caution that the assignments might not be definite, while calling the product primarily birnessite. Their reference-pattern formula is not a measured batch formula or quantified phase purity. The chemically prepared comparator is assigned to ramsdellite, not abiotic birnessite. Figure 2 reports 39.2 versus 54.1 wt% Mn and retained organics; Figure 3 FTIR supports organic functional groups; Figure 4 shows porous/hypha-associated versus more uniform particles; Figure 5 contrasts pore structure. BET areas are 81.4 versus 11.3 m²/g. Figure 6 assesses surface charge in 1 mM KNO3. These coupled material differences confound a causal claim that biological origin alone improves sorption.

**Sorption stage (section 2.3, Figure 7).** The experiment used prepared freeze-dried powder, not growing cultures: 0.020 g composite or 0.015 g chemical oxide in 0.150 L solution, nominal solid Mn 1 mmol/L, Co nitrate, 100 mmol/L KNO3, PIPES-buffered initial pH 6.5, 25 °C and 100 rpm. Aqueous Co and Mn were measured after 0.2 µm filtration by AAS/ICP-MS; time courses were duplicated. Matched lyophilized fungal tissue alone showed no observed Co uptake. Both oxide curves declined, with slower sorption and lower residual Co for the biogenic arm in the displayed assay. The microbial flag concerns biogenic sorbent origin and retained biomass; no viable-cell or ongoing-metabolism requirement was demonstrated.

**Quantity and timing limits.** Methods specify initial Co up to 175 µmol/L, but Figure 7 starts around 0.21 mmol/L and the prose additionally mentions an unshown 0.55 mmol/L assay. Methods say 100–300 hours, while displayed curves extend past 300 hours. The reported normalized values 160 and 28.7 mmol Co/mol solid Mn and their 5.6-fold ratio cannot readily be reproduced from the displayed concentration changes and nominal 1 mmol/L solid Mn. They are retained as a source-calculation discrepancy, not accepted capacities or a fold advantage. The printed 500 dm³ sorption-flask unit and implausibly concentrated chemical-comparator recipe are not silently corrected into measured experimental values. The reported instrument detection limits use µg/L, not mg/L; no censored-value or quantitative-observation fields are proposed.

**Redox and control boundary (Figures 8–11).** Figure 8 includes no-Co controls showing background Mn release. Figure 11 subtracts that background; its Mn ordinate is not total dissolution. Figure 9 compares surface Mn XPS before/after. Figure 10's Co 2p evidence is interpreted by the authors as surface Co(III), with Mn 2s interference requiring attention to Co 2p1/2. Sorption solids were air-dried one week at 4 °C before XPS; this is ex situ surface evidence, not an identified bulk CoOOH product, total oxidation fraction or closed redox balance. The abstract prioritizes Mn(IV) reaction over Mn(III); the body reverses that preference. Neither is adopted as a settled Mn oxidant mechanism. No isolated cobalt recovery product, regeneration/desorption yield or industrial deployment is demonstrated.
