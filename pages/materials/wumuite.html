Wumuite mineral

Wumuite (IMA 2017-067a) is an IMA-accepted potassium-aluminium-tungsten oxide mineral with a hexagonal tungsten bronze (HTB)-type structure (Strunz 04.E, Dana 07 AB3O9) and September 2026 IMA-CNMNC Master List ideal formula KAl0.33W2.67O9 (PDF p. 236, status A; also written K(W2.67Al0.33)O9 or K(W2.67Al0.33)Sigma3O9 in Xue, Li, and Xie 2020). Described by Xue, Li, and Xie (2020) from a lightly weathered yellowish-gray aplitic Neoproterozoic Sinian (800 Ma zircon age) biotite–quartz monzonite body near the contact zone of Permian mafic rocks and ~300 m northeast of a fault-controlled pyrite-quartz-vein gold deposit in gabbro, near Nanyang village, Huaping County, Lijiang City, Yunnan Province, adjacent to northwestern Panzhihua City on the Sichuan–Yunnan boundary in the Panzhihua–Xichang (Pan–Xi) region, southwestern China (note coordinate discrepancy between the Abstract on p. 483, 26°46'8.21" N, 101°27'13.86" E, and Section 2 on p. 484, 26°46'18.21" N, 101°27'13.86" E; named after the Wumu River; holotype M13782 at the Geological Museum of China, Beijing, and cotype NY-6-2Z at the Crystal Structure Laboratory, China University of Geosciences, Beijing, pp. 483–485). Occurs as light green, transparent, hexagonal tabular crystals up to 0.3 mm across dominated by {0001} pinacoids with {10-10}, {01-10}, and {1-100} prism forms, associated with major quartz, orthoclase, albite, biotite, hornblende, and kaolinite, minor/accessory ilmenite, goethite, hematite, zircon, zoisite, tourmaline, monazite-(Ce), and allanite-(Ce), and W–Te mineralization comprising earlier automorphic scheelite inclusions, tellurite, later allotriomorphic rims and intergrowths of tewite (K1.5(Te,W)1.5W5O19, IMA 2014-053), and unnamed natural WO3 (pp. 483–485, Figs. 1–3). Exhibits a white streak, vitreous to adamantine lustre, brittle tenacity, good {10-10} and {0001} cleavage, Mohs hardness ~5–6, Dcalc = 6.52 g/cm3 (measured density not determined due to small crystal size), uniaxial (+) optics with Gladstone–Dale calculated refractive index n = 2.13, and reflection FTIR bands (Bruker HYPERION 1000, 4000–400 cm-1, Section 5, pp. 485–486, Fig. 4) at 816 cm-1 (WO6 octahedral stretching) and 523, 475, 446, and 424 cm-1 (W–O bending). Ten-point WDS electron-microprobe analysis on a single grain (JEOL JXA-8100, 20 kV, 10 nA, 2 um beam; Section 4, p. 485, Table 1) yielded WO3 91.16 wt% (range 88.45–92.35, SD 1.10, scheelite standard), K2O 5.55 (5.16–5.85, SD 0.28, microcline standard), Al2O3 2.52 (2.40–2.68, SD 0.04, albite standard), TeO2 0.59 (0.54–0.75, SD 0.08, Sb2Te3 standard), total 99.82 wt%, corresponding on 9 O apfu to the empirical formula K0.80(W2.68Al0.34Te0.03)Sigma3.05O9 within the nonstoichiometric HTB solid-solution series Kx(W3-x/3Alx/3)Sigma3O9 (x <= 1, coupled substitution 3[] + W6+ = 3K+ + Al3+). Hexagonal, space group P6/mmm, Z = 1 (Sections 6.1–6.2, pp. 485–492, Tables 2–7, Figs. 5–8): primary single-crystal MoKalpha XRD refinement on a 0.07 x 0.07 x 0.05 mm crystal (Bruker SMART APEX-CCD, 162 reflections with I > 2sigma(I) out of 164 independent, Rint = 0.01, R1 = 0.025, wR2 = 0.072, GoF = 1.05, Table 3, p. 488) gives a = 7.2952(5) Å, c = 3.7711(3) Å (printed as 3.7713 Å in the Abstract on p. 483 and Section 6.2 prose on p. 487), V = 173.81(2) Å3, with corner-sharing (W0.89Al0.11)O6 octahedra at 3f (mean (W,Al)–O = 1.923 Å) forming hexagonal tunnels along c partially occupied by K1 at 1b (occupancy 0.8, K1–O2 = 3.182(2) Å); a second SCXRD measurement (Rigaku XtaLAB PRO-007HF, MoKalpha, Section 6.2, p. 487) gave a = 7.2904(2) Å, c = 3.7686(2) Å, V = 173.47(1) Å3 for the 1c subcell and revealed weak satellite reflections doubling c (c' = 2c = 7.5396(5) Å, a = 7.2904(3) Å, V = 347.09(2) Å3 in P6/mmm, or C-centered orthorhombic a = 7.2848(4) Å, b = 12.6348(6) Å, c = 7.5395(4) Å, V = 693.96(6) Å3) due to alternating K occupancy (1.0 and 0.56 at 1a and 1b in the 2c superstructure); CHECKCELL powder XRD refinement (MoKalpha, Section 6.1, p. 485, Table 2, p. 487) gave a = 7.285(5) Å, c = 3.767(1) Å, V = 173.1(3) Å3, with four strongest lines at 3.161 (100)(020), 2.413 (40)(021), 6.261 (36)(010), and 3.727 (30)(001) Å. In abiotic solid-state synthesis experiments (Section 7, pp. 492–493, Fig. 9), heating pure WO3 with natural granite orthoclase (KAlSi3O8) at 700 °C and 1 atm yielded synthetic wumuite, and heating synthetic wumuite with WO3 and TeO2 for 10 h at 650 °C and 1 atm yielded synthetic tewite. Distinct from tewite, liguowuite, scheelite, tellurite, and elemental tungsten; no criticality designation for wumuite itself, no microbial or biological mechanism, and no industrial recovery yield are asserted.
