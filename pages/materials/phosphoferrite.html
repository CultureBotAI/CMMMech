Phosphoferrite mineral

Phosphoferrite is the ferrous phosphate mineral with ideal formula Fe2+3(PO4)2(H2O)3. The September 2026 IMA list records redefined status under the 1980 special procedure. It is distinct from the more hydrated species vivianite and ludlamite, the Mn-dominant related mineral reddingite, and ferric kryzhanovskite. The historical Hagendorf Sud specimen studied by Moore (1971) contained Mn and Ca and was not a pure synthetic Fe endmember. Laboratory heating produced a source-reported ferric crystalline product with diffraction similarities to the starting material. Later structural work by Moore, Araki and Kampf (1980) corrected substantial indexing and unit-cell errors in the earlier natural kryzhanovskite comparison; this record therefore retains the limited heating observation without treating the earlier cell dimensions, exact structural model or pure-product identity as established. These are abiotic laboratory observations, not microbial extraction or demonstrated industrial recovery. No criticality designation is inherited from iron or phosphorus.

Microbial & Biochemical Mechanisms (1)

Abiotic heating-associated oxidation of natural phosphoferrite

redox transformation

Moore (1971) reports that Hagendorf Sud phosphoferrite heated in air at 240 degrees C for five days oxidized to a ferric equivalent while retaining single-crystal and powder diffraction features interpreted as isotypic with the initial material, with broadened lines and spots. This establishes a qualified thermal oxidation observation for the investigated natural specimen. It does not establish a mass-balanced yield of pure kryzhanovskite, a measured hydrogen-production pathway or a microbial process. The proposed internal proton-cleavage mechanism was a hypothesis; the author specifically proposed anaerobic heating and quantitative hydrogen collection as a future test.

Experimental Context & Analytical Limits:
Natural phosphoferrite crystals from Hagendorf Sud, supplied by D. J. Fisher; Table 1 uses a historical chemical analysis with Mn and Ca substitution. Crystals of phosphoferrite, vivianite and ludlamite were heated separately in open capillaries at 120, 180 and 240 degrees C for five days, with a chromel-alumel thermocouple in contact with the crystals. Products were quenched to room temperature, dissolved in cold HCl and titrated against potassium dichromate; diffraction was compared with starting material. The explicit phosphoferrite outcome is reported at 240 degrees C; lower-temperature phosphoferrite outcomes, sample masses, replicate counts, quantitative conversion uncertainty and a mass balance are not supplied in this experiment description. Vivianite and ludlamite are different-mineral comparisons, not matched oxygen-free phosphoferrite controls. The 1980 study revises the earlier natural kryzhanovskite reference data, not this heating experiment, and does not independently replicate its outcome.
