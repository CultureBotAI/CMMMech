Electroplating sludge secondary resource

Metal-bearing solids generated by treatment of electroplating wastewater, represented here by the Chennai effluent-treatment sludge of Prabhu and Baskar (2015) and the compositionally different electroplating sludge of Kordloo et al. (2026). The latter paper does not name its sludge supplier or sampling site. These heterogeneous wastes are not pure metal compounds, untreated plating baths, metallic electroforming scrap, GaAs-production hydroxide sludge, or the existing automotive-factory sludge whose contributing process is unresolved. The two studies are separate assays, not pooled feeds or a replication. No constituent-element criticality is inherited.

Microbial & Biochemical Mechanisms (2)

Culture-associated acidification and zinc/nickel solubilization

bioleaching Microbial

Associated Organisms: Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans (NCBITaxon:920) [TaxonMech]

A sludge-acclimatized, source-assigned Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans isolate in sulfur medium was associated with acidification and greater aqueous Zn/Ni release than the paper's uninoculated, thymol-treated comparison. This supports conditional laboratory leaching, without establishing a unique biological rate-controlling reaction or recovered metal product.

Experimental Context & Analytical Limits:
Prabhu and Baskar sampled an electroplating-sector effluent-treatment sludge bed in Chennai at 2-5 cm depth, refrigerated it and air-dried it overnight. Table 2 gives dry-feed Zn 42,180±685 mg/kg and Ni 20,160±430 mg/kg (five samples, mean±SD). An acid-mine-drainage isolate from Chitradurga was assigned to A. ferrooxidans using reported physiological/morphological traits; no accession or strain deposit is supplied. It was acclimatized through 0.25-1% sludge. Triplicate 250 mL flasks contained 90 mL sulfur medium plus 10 mL inoculum, 1-5% (w/v) sludge, initial pH 3 adjusted with sulfuric acid, 28 °C and 150 rpm for 480 h. The 1% uninoculated control additionally received 5% (v/v) alcoholic thymol solution (1% w/v thymol in alcohol). AAS measured filtered supernatant from 5 mL withdrawals every 48 h; sulfur-free fresh medium replaced sampling/evaporation loss. These controls are not chemically matched or pH-matched across the incubation.

Curated Evidence Points:
Support Source: prabhu2015
Methods pp. 1250-1251 and Figures 2-4 support culture-associated acidification and aqueous metal mobilization. The 1% treatment ended at pH 1.17 versus 2.63 in the uninoculated control. Results pp. 1252-1253 report Zn/Ni leaching percentages 96.31/84.40 at 1% solids and 79.03/66.14 at 5% after 20 days; Figure 4 shows substantially smaller nonzero control release. These are source-reported assay percentages, not independently reconstructed mass recoveries.
Partial Source: prabhu2015
Printed Equation 4 uses (M0−Msoln)/MT although its definitions describe increasing aqueous metal at time t; the solid/liquid normalization and corrections for repeated sampling are not resolved. Thus the percentages retain a calculation limitation. Only the 1% loading has the stated uninoculated control, which also differs by thymol/alcohol; lower pH and mobilization do not isolate a contribution beyond acid leaching. No sequence-based identification, mineral-phase-resolved reaction, purified product, complete mass balance, or industrial deployment is demonstrated.
Partial Source: prabhu2015
Figures 7-10 and Tables 3-4 are model fits, not independent mechanistic proof. Several Ni regression coefficients in Table 4 disagree with the original plotted labels, and Table 4 gives higher liquid-film than chemical-reaction R² for 2-4% solids. The paper's universal chemical-reaction-control conclusion is therefore not adopted. Table 2 reports organic matter 4.1±0.11%, whereas the prose gives 4.1 mg/kg; the medium prints Ca(CO3)2. These source inconsistencies are not silently corrected or used to infer nutrient or phase mechanisms.

Ultrasound-assisted leaching with culture-generated ferric acidic liquor

bioleaching Microbial

Associated Organisms: Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans (NCBITaxon:920) [TaxonMech]

Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans was used upstream to prepare acidic ferric liquor; after centrifugation, the source-described cell-free reagent leached Cr, Cu and Ni from electroplating sludge. Ultrasound comparisons concern this subsequent chemical leaching stage. They do not demonstrate living-cell contact with sludge or an advantage over chemically equivalent acid and ferric iron.

Experimental Context & Analytical Limits:
Kordloo et al. report a feed containing 8.23% Cr, 5.97% Cu and 10.92% Ni; supplier/site and detailed generation history are not given. The culture came from the Soil and Water Research Institute, Karaj, without a strain identifier. Sulfur/ferrous medium was initially acidified to pH 1.8; 100 mL cultures with 10% inoculum were shaken for one week at 34 °C and 140 rpm. The final liquor was reported at pH 1.2, 650 mV ORP and 9 g/L ferric iron, then centrifuged 15 min at 5000 rpm. A 100 mL portion was used in 250 mL three-neck leaching flasks. A 37 kHz, 150 W ultrasonic bath was tested against no ultrasound; OFAT assays varied 300-500 rpm, 10-50 g/L solids and ambient to 75 °C, with triplicate experiments. Initial acid addition and culture-derived iron remain part of the reagent; centrifugation alone is the reported cell-removal step.

Curated Evidence Points:
Support Source: kordloo2026
Methods pp. 2-4, Figure 1 and Supplementary Figure S3 establish upstream culture preparation followed by cell-removed reagent leaching. Figure 1 shows an early ultrasonic benefit for Ni/Cr at ambient temperature, 10 g/L and 400 rpm; Cu differences are small and the prose explicitly reports no significant Cu effect. Longer-time differences narrow. This is a conditional ultrasound result using biogenic reagent, not proof of a unique microbial extraction advantage.
Partial Source: kordloo2026
Figure 4 and results pp. 7-8 report 92.51% Cr, 86.91% Cu and 91.25% Ni at 8 min, 45 °C, 400 rpm and 10 g/L. Higher temperature and later times reduce reported dissolved fractions in several arms; ambient-temperature endpoints can exceed the 8 min optimum, so this is not a universal maximum. The paper provides no explicit extraction equation or complete sampling/mass balance; Table S2 reports final-PLS concentrations in ppm without sufficient linkage to reconstruct these percentages. No isolated saleable product or plant-scale operation is shown.
Partial Source: kordloo2026
A chemistry-matched sterile H2SO4/ferric-iron control is absent, explicitly proposed as future work on p. 11. Figures 5/S5 support source-assigned hydronium-jarosite-containing residues and surface-composition changes; they do not directly measure Cr/Cu/Ni incorporation into that phase or establish the proposed passivation/rate-control pathway. Medium descriptions disagree on 44.22 versus 44.7 g/L and print Fe2SO4·7H2O versus FeSO4·7H2O. Table 4 lists six solids levels including 25 g/L, while Figure 3 displays five without 25 g/L; these are not harmonized.
Refute Source: kordloo2026
The broad abstract claim that both toxicity tests demonstrate detoxification is contradicted by p. 10 and Figure S6a: residue Ni is 13.9 mg/L, above the source's 5 mg/L TCLP comparator. Figure S6b labels the SPLP Ni threshold not stated, despite prose claiming both metals fall below thresholds. These limited extraction tests do not establish general detoxification or safe landfill disposal; source comparator values are not independently adopted regulatory limits.
Context_only Source: ncbi920
NCBI confirms the species label/identifier used by both papers. It cannot authenticate either unnamed experimental culture or resolve their purity, strain, or molecular mechanism.
