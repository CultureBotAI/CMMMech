Thulium element Critical Material

Thulium is the element Tm, represented by its neutral-atom identity and distinct from Tm(III) chloride solutions, mineral hosts and rare-earth commodities. It is named in the United States Final 2025 List of Critical Minerals. Prepared Bacillus subtilis cell powders provide laboratory evidence for thulium biosorption and subsequent acidic elution, without an isolated pure-metal yield or demonstrated industrial process.

Microbial & Biochemical Mechanisms (1)

Thulium biosorption by boiled and freeze-dried Bacillus subtilis cell powders

biosorption Microbial

Associated Organisms: Bacillus subtilis subsp. subtilis str. 168 (NCBITaxon:224308)

Inactive cell powders from B. subtilis 168 and its LTA-defective RY1604 derivative removed dissolved thulium from defined chloride solutions. Table 3 entry 4 reports 90% and 74% removal for WT and LTA-defective powders, respectively, at 20 micrograms Tm/mL. Removal persisted in the defective preparation, so the comparison does not establish an exclusive lipoteichoic-acid-mediated route or quantify its contribution. Nitric acid eluted adsorbed rare-earth ions in a separate recovery step; the study did not establish a purified thulium-metal product.

Experimental Context & Analytical Limits:
The accepted manuscript describes RY1604 as deletions of yfnI, yqgS and yvgJ combined with inducible ltaS, prepared after IPTG removal; Table 1 uses yvyJ where the construction methods use yvgJ. Biomass was boiled for 20 minutes, washed, freeze-dried, crushed and sieved to 53–100 micrometres. Assays used 20 mg powder in 20 mL metal solution at pH 3, 25 degrees C, mixed at 10 rpm for 30 minutes. Filter paper with nominal 7-micrometre retention and subsequent centrifugation separated solids before ICP atomic-emission spectrometry. Filter-associated adsorption was reported below 10%, but a blank-correction calculation and replicated uncertainty for Table 3 were not specified. WT-versus-mutant comparison tests these prepared biomasses, not growing-cell metabolism. No complemented-mutant adsorption assay was reported. The fitted saturation capacities are not accepted as measured capacities here: the deposited manuscript refers to Table 4 but does not include that table. The experiments used laboratory synthetic solutions, not ore or field water.
Curated Evidence Points:
Support Source: moriwaki_accepted_manuscript
Preparation and Adsorption test methods (PDF pp. 7–9), Table 3 (PDF p. 21) and Figure 2 (PDF p. 23) support rapid Tm solution depletion by both powders. The Table 3 concentration header is micrograms/mL in the rendered PDF, despite erroneous extracted text. Entry 6 reports lower fractional removal at 100 micrograms/mL (35% WT; 29% defective), so the 20-micrograms/mL outcome is not a loading-independent performance guarantee.
Partial Source: moriwaki_accepted_manuscript
The results on PDF p. 11 describe two 20-mL elutions with 2.7 M nitric acid and greater than 94% recovery of adsorbed rare-earth ions for both powders. This is stated across La, Eu and Tm, with no element-specific elution table or uncertainty; it is not greater than 94% recovery of initial thulium feed. Morphology, residual binding in RY1604 and the unquantified LTA/WTA abundances prevent assigning an exclusive ligand mechanism. Both Langmuir and Freundlich fits were acceptable, and the authors explicitly say these fits do not distinguish the adsorption mechanism. No direct Tm speciation or site-resolved spectroscopy is supplied.
Context_only Source: moriwaki_2013
Publisher metadata and abstract confirm the publication identity and broad WT/defective comparison. The full scientific assessment uses the openly deposited accepted manuscript; the subscription version and its referenced capacity table were not accessed.
