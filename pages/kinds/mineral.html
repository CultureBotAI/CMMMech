Alburnite

Alburnite is the natural silver-germanium sulfide-telluride mineral Ag8GeTe2S4, listed as approved IMA 2012-073 in the September 2026 IMA-CNMNC list. The original 2013 approval announcement identifies the Cârnicel vein, Roşia Montană deposit, Apuseni Mountains, Romania, as its type locality. A 2016 study of a Măgura vein specimen reported an arsenic-bearing phase as alburnite using optical microscopy and SEM-EDS, but explicitly left its identity unresolved: it could have alburnite symmetry or be a distinct phase. Baddeleyite

Baddeleyite is the natural monoclinic zirconium oxide mineral species ZrO2, listed as grandfathered in the September 2026 IMA-CNMNC list. It is distinct from elemental zirconium, zircon (ZrSiO4), synthetic zirconia products and other ZrO2 polymorphs. Huraiová, Hurai and Konečný (2011) characterized baddeleyite associated with a zoned zircon crystal separated from redeposited sediments of the Hajnáčka-Kostná dolina basaltic maar, southern Slovakia. Cathodoluminescence and backscattered-electron images show baddeleyite in melt pockets and in a reaction rim intergrown with sanidine and anorthoclase. Their Table 2 reports six representative baddeleyite microprobe analyses containing 96.65-98.27 wt.% ZrO2 and 0.99-1.06 wt.% HfO2, alongside analyses of the distinct host zircon. These oxide-equivalent specimen compositions are not elemental-metal percentages, mineral-wide purity or resource grades. The study uses microscopy and electron microprobe chemistry; it does not report a new diffraction refinement. Formation by reaction of zircon with Si-poor, Al-rich melt is the authors' geological interpretation, not a controlled transformation experiment. The reported absence of light rare earth elements is bounded by analytical detection limits, not proof of zero concentration. This identity and natural-occurrence evidence does not establish microbial extraction, recovery, industrial deployment or a dated criticality designation for the mineral.

Beudantite

Beudantite is the lead ferric arsenate-sulfate hydroxide mineral with ideal formula PbFe3(AsO4)(SO4)(OH)6, distinct from phosphate-bearing corkite, plumbojarosite, segnitite and elemental lead or arsenic. The September 2026 IMA list records its redefinition by special procedure in 1987; that date is not an individual approval accession. Szymański (1988) examined compositionally zoned natural material from Tsumeb, Namibia. Electron-microprobe analyses showed variable Al substitution for Fe and variable As/S ratios rather than a uniform ideal endmember. The accepted single-crystal refinement was crystal 2, with a = 7.3151(9) and c = 17.0355(5) angstrom, R-3m symmetry, 644 unique reflections and a final R factor of 3.7 percent. It supported disordered As/S sites and displaced Pb positions. Crystal 1 had unresolved diffraction-profile problems; crystal 3 showed a slight triclinic distortion but could not be satisfactorily refined and its composition was not determined. Neither the preferred symmetry nor the proposed Pb lone-pair explanation is generalized to every specimen. The article's separately deposited structure-factor Table 4 was not acquired; the published experimental methods, Tables 1-3/5 and Figures 1-3 support this bounded identity assessment. Bari et al. (2024) provide adjacent abiotic dissolution context for a synthetic solid they call beudantite. Its reported bulk formula, Pb0.32(H3O)0.68Fe3(AsO4)0.32(SO4)1.68(OH)6, differs markedly from the ideal species, and initial TEM-EDX detected grains lacking both As and Pb. Their 56-day tests at initial pH 2, 4, 6 and 8 used 0.06 g solid in 40 mL solution at 22 degrees C with triplicate sacrificial sampling. Filtered aqueous element contents, residual diffraction and arsenic X-ray absorption measurements describe that substituted, heterogeneous preparation. Greater mobilization at pH 2 and persistent residual diffraction do not establish dissolution rates or long-term stability of pure natural beudantite. No phase-specific dissolution mechanism is assigned here from those data. The source's inconsistent Fe unit, duplicated sulfate in its reaction equation, and comparison to an ideal Fe/Pb ratio despite the measured composition are not adopted as quantitative or balanced-reaction claims. These sources do not establish microbial transformation, isolated-product recovery or industrial operation. No constituent-element criticality is inherited.

Carnotite

Carnotite is the potassium uranyl vanadate mineral K2(UO2)2(VO4)2·3H2O. The September 2026 IMA-CNMNC list recognizes it with grandfathered status G; 1899 is the original-description year, not an IMA approval accession. This mineral-species owner is distinct from the broader carnotite group, elemental uranium or vanadium, mixed uranium ore and dissolved uranyl or vanadate salts. Glasauer et al. (2022) identified K-carnotite together with metatyuyamunite by X-ray diffraction in the final fine fraction of a natural uranium-vanadium sandstone incubated with living Shewanella putrefaciens CN32. The starting assemblage was identified as metatyuyamunite and fritzscheite, rather than a pure carnotite feed. Figure 1b and sections 3.1 and 3.3 support this bounded phase-occurrence comparison. Increased K and decreased Ca in the final fine fraction are consistent with interlayer-cation exchange. The study does not isolate a uniquely biological cause of carnotite formation: the authors recognize both biotic and abiotic pathways, and do not provide a matched final abiotic diffraction series. Section 3.2 describes tyuyamunite-to-carnotite conversion whereas the conclusion names fritzscheite as the precursor; this record assigns neither unique precursor. The shared laboratory experiment used 1.6 g of mixed sandstone in 80 mL defined medium with 10 mM sodium lactate and 1 mM phosphate, nitrogen degassing and autoclaving before inoculation, followed by an anoxic glovebox incubation extending to ten months. Treatments were in triplicate. These three mineral records refer to the same experiment, not independent replications. Bacteria-free solution controls were included, but do not isolate each phase's fate. The mixed-ore spectra support partial V reduction while U remained predominantly U(VI); results from the separate soluble-salt arm must not be transferred to this mineral. Regerite is a hydrated potassium ferric phosphate mineral with ideal formula KFe6(PO4)4(OH)7(H2O)6·4H2O, approved as IMA 2023-028 and retained in the September 2026 IMA list. Rewitzer et al. The natural material contains titanium; wavelength-dispersive analyses of 15 crystals are not independent occurrences or a synthesis experiment. Water was calculated from the structural model and an assumed charge-compensating substitution, rather than directly measured. Single-crystal synchrotron diffraction at 100 K supports monoclinic P21/c symmetry and a framework of iron-centred octahedra and phosphate tetrahedra with potassium and water in channels. Bond-valence sums support the ferric assignment, rather than a separate direct iron-valence assay. Twenty-five of the 27 ideal hydrogen positions were located and refined with restraints; H22 and H30b remained unresolved. The deposited all-Fe model and ideal formula do not represent the full measured titanium-bearing composition. The proposed titanium distribution, displacement and charge-compensation scheme remain interpretations, with displaced Ti not unambiguously confirmed. The text's O18-to-O33 water-site range overlaps its hydroxyl assignment; Table 4 and the deposited hydrogen model instead distinguish O17-to-O23 hydroxyl sites from O24-to-O33 water sites. Natural occurrence and crystal structure do not establish a formation, microbial extraction or recovery mechanism. No criticality designation is inherited from constituent elements.

Svanbergite

Svanbergite is the strontium aluminium sulfate-phosphate mineral SrAl3(SO4)(PO4)(OH)6, distinct from calcium-dominant woodhouseite, strontium aluminium phosphate goyazite and strontium as an element. Single-crystal diffraction used 283 reflections and supported R-3m symmetry, a=6.992(2) and c=16.567(5) angstrom, with R=0.085. This specimen-specific structure contains disordered phosphorus/sulfur tetrahedral sites; the paper does not provide a new chemical analysis of that exact crystal or locate all hydrogen atoms directly. Svanbergite-rich grains accompany alunite or kaolinite in shallower Summitville material, while La Escondida includes intermediate Ca/Sr compositions assigned probable hypogene or supergene origins from textures. Figure 1 shows a grain with subequal Ca and Sr rather than a pure svanbergite endmember; Table 3 and Figure 2 retain compositional variation and calculated water. Replacement or dissolution of apatite in acidic, sulfate-rich alteration is a geological interpretation, not a time-resolved transformation assay or demonstrated microbial mechanism. The 250 degrees C stability diagram in Figure 3 is a calcium-system woodhouseite inference and must not be treated as an experimentally measured svanbergite stability field. The companion woodhouseite record uses the same field-study lineage. Mineral identity and qualified natural alteration context are supported; microbial formation, strontium recovery yield, industrial deployment and mineral-specific criticality are not asserted.

Woodhouseite

Woodhouseite is the calcium aluminium sulfate-phosphate mineral CaAl3(SO4)(PO4)(OH)6, distinct from strontium-dominant svanbergite, calcium aluminium phosphate crandallite and calcium as an element. The September 2026 IMA list records its redefinition by special procedure in 1987. Lemmon (1937) described natural crystals from quartz veins adjoining and penetrating andalusite deposits on White Mountain, Mono County, California. Geometrical crystallography, optical observations and wet chemical analysis of hand-sorted crystals supported the new mineral. Its analysis contains minor substituents and a phosphate-sulfate imbalance; Lemmon explicitly considered a single analysis insufficient to prove the proposed substitution. This historical description is not a modern single-crystal refinement. Stoffregen and Alpers (1987) subsequently measured natural aluminium phosphate-sulfate grains by electron microprobe at Summitville, La Granja and La Escondida. Calcium-rich grains approaching woodhouseite occur at depth in Summitville and at La Granja, where small grains appear to replace apatite and contact pyrophyllite. Variable Ca/Sr and phosphate/sulfate ratios, minor additional cations and calculated rather than measured water distinguish these grains from pure ideal endmembers. Figure 1 depicts an approximately equal Ca/Sr grain, not pure woodhouseite. Apatite dissolution or replacement during advanced argillic alteration is the authors' field-textural interpretation; there was no controlled formation experiment, reaction time series or microbial assay. Their 250 degrees C, 40-bar stability diagram uses assumed aqueous compositions and an inferred woodhouseite boundary because direct thermodynamic data were unavailable to that study. It is not a measured formation threshold, and its hydroxyapatite model omits fluoride/chloride effects. The companion svanbergite record uses the same field study. These sources establish mineral identity and qualified natural alteration context, not microbial formation, elemental extraction yield, isolated-product recovery or industrial deployment. No constituent-element criticality is inherited.

Hazen and Finger (1979) characterized two fragments of one natural, nonmetamict, gem-quality crystal from the Finsch Pipe kimberlite near Kimberley, South Africa, using single-crystal X-ray diffraction under pressure. Their specimen description reports nearly pure zircon with 1.2 weight percent HfO2 and 26 ppm uranium; these are specimen-specific values, not universal mineral composition or a resource grade. The refinements support a tetragonal I41/amd structure with four formula units per unit cell. Measurements extended to 48.1 kbar; higher-pressure phase transitions discussed in that paper are predictions or references to other studies, not transformations observed in this experiment. The two fragments are not independent geological occurrences. This identity and structural evidence does not establish microbial transformation, recovery, industrial deployment or a criticality designation for the mineral.

Zirconolite

Zirconolite is a zirconium-bearing oxide mineral listed by IMA-CNMNC in September 2026 with formula (Ca,Y)Zr(Ti,Mg,Al)2O7 and redefined status. Its 1989 special-procedure entry is not a numbered new-mineral approval. CaZrTi2O7 is the simplified composition used in the cited nomenclature and natural-specimen study, not the measured formula of every grain. The IMA-approved nomenclature published by Bayliss et al. (1989) uses the unsuffixed name for metamict or undetermined-polytypoid material and distinguishes the 2M, 3T and 3O layered variants from cubic zirkelite. This record does not assign a particular polytypoid, space group or synthetic ceramic composition to an uncharacterized natural specimen. Bellatreccia et al. (1999) studied a natural polycrystalline aggregate collected at Jacupiranga, São Paulo, Brazil, from the locality historically associated with the name zirkelite. It was explicitly a different specimen from the historical type specimen. One hand-picked grain yielded no diffraction pattern after 48 hours in a Gandolfi camera; the authors assigned zirconolite using the metamict state and chemical composition. Three crystals examined by electron microscopy and electron microprobe showed oscillatory, sector or irregular compositional zoning. Their zirconium-bearing chemistry also included niobium, thorium, uranium, hafnium and rare-earth elements; this is specimen chemistry rather than a bulk resource grade.
