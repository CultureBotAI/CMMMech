Alburnite

Alburnite is the natural silver-germanium sulfide-telluride mineral Ag8GeTe2S4, listed as approved IMA 2012-073 in the September 2026 IMA-CNMNC list. The original 2013 approval announcement identifies the Cârnicel vein, Roşia Montană deposit, Apuseni Mountains, Romania, as its type locality. This species remains distinct from argyrodite and from generic Te-bearing material historically called argyrodite. A 2016 study of a Măgura vein specimen reported an arsenic-bearing phase as alburnite using optical microscopy and SEM-EDS, but explicitly left its identity unresolved: it could have alburnite symmetry or be a distinct phase. Its nonstoichiometric, silver-excess EDS result is not treated here as a confirmed alburnite composition. That paper also corrects the authors' 2015 report of associated Cu-Fe-bearing sulfotellurides, attributing the earlier signals to a SEM charge- compensation artifact involving neighboring chalcopyrite; this is not a correction of the 2014 alburnite description. The complete 2014 description and its graphics were not available for this curation, so their quantitative and structural detail remains deferred. No extraction mechanism, recovery performance or constituent-element criticality designation is assigned to this mineral.

Argyrodite

Argyrodite is the silver-germanium sulfide mineral species Ag8GeS6, listed by IMA-CNMNC in September 2026 as grandfathered, with 1886 as the original-description year rather than an IMA approval number. Ono and Sato's 1995 study identified natural argyrodite in fine-grained massive black ore from the Ezuri Kuroko deposit, Japan, using reflected-light microscopy and electron-microprobe analysis. Figure 3F shows the mineral with galena, covellite, chalcopyrite and bornite in barite; Table 2 gives a specimen analysis described as close to ideal Ag8GeS6, with minor Cu, Fe and As. This specimen observation does not establish uniform composition, bulk germanium grade, resource size or recovery yield. The authors' fluid-inclusion measurements concern sphalerite and their formation-temperature interpretation is not an argyrodite extraction assay. This natural species is distinct from synthetic lithium argyrodite electrolytes and from the separately recognized alburnite, Ag8GeTe2S4. Historical Te-rich argyrodite reports from Roşia Montană are not used here as interchangeable composition evidence: the 2014 alburnite description explicitly revisits the earlier interpretation. Handbook crystallographic citations to synthetic Ag8GeS6 are not treated as measurements of the Ezuri specimen. No microbial mechanism, industrial recovery performance or inherited constituent-element criticality designation is assigned.

Briartite

Briartite is the copper-iron-germanium sulfide mineral species Cu2FeGeS4, listed as approved, IMA 1965-018, by IMA-CNMNC in September 2026. Francotte and colleagues' 1965 description used the broader historical formula Cu2(Fe,Zn)GeS4 and combined polished-section observations, powder X-ray diffraction and electron-microprobe analyses of natural material from Kipushi, with comparative Tsumeb chemistry. Its Fe-Zn variation and uncertain structural interpretation do not establish that every historical analysis belongs to the currently delimited Fe species. The dated IMA list separately recognizes zincobriartite, Cu2ZnGeS4, IMA 2015-094; zinc-rich historical material is not silently reassigned to this briartite record. The original describes small grains and intergrowths with other sulfides, including chalcopyrite, renierite and sphalerite, rather than a pure bulk ore feed. Its Table III column labels conflict with the accompanying prose, so individual assay rows are not imported as unambiguous specimen measurements. Briartite is distinct from germanium element, germanite, renierite and a mixed copper-zinc ore. These mineralogical observations establish neither a microbial recovery mechanism nor industrial extraction performance; no constituent-element criticality designation is inherited.

Dzhalindite

Dzhalindite is the indium hydroxide mineral species In(OH)3, listed as approved in the September 2026 IMA-CNMNC list with the special-procedure year 1967. It is distinct from elemental indium, indite (FeIn2S4), mixed ores and synthetic indium hydroxide products considered only by formula. Kiseleva and colleagues (2008) report a natural occurrence in one quartz-sulfide vein section from 50 metres below the surface at the Bugdaya deposit, eastern Transbaikalia, Russia. They describe 30-94 micrometre crystals in late quartz and zoned In-bearing grains examined by optical microscopy, backscattered-electron imaging and energy-dispersive elemental analysis. Their dzhalindite assignment rests on composition, optical properties and apparent crystal symmetry; X-ray diffraction was unsuccessful because the segregations were too small. Observed Fe zoning does not establish iron speciation or prove substitution within the mineral lattice. The suggested hypogene origin and precipitation from fluids that corroded sphalerite are interpretations, not demonstrated reaction pathways. Temperatures inferred from other phases or analogous veins are not direct measurements on these crystals. This particular occurrence does not establish a universal origin, a bulk resource grade, or an extraction yield. No microbial mechanism, industrial recovery or constituent-derived criticality is assigned.

Germanite

Germanite is the copper-iron-germanium sulfide mineral species Cu13Fe2Ge2S16, listed by IMA-CNMNC in September 2026 as grandfathered, with 1922 as the original-description year rather than an IMA approval number. The 1984 powder-XRD study of Tsumeb material uses the equivalent doubled formula Cu26Ge4Fe4S32 and supports a cubic structure, while explicitly retaining intergrown impurities and unresolved cation-site assignments. Its ideal formula does not establish the composition or purity of every specimen. The Handbook describes occurrence in dolostone-hosted hydrothermal copper-lead-zinc ore at Tsumeb and association with renierite and other sulfides. These are geological observations, not current production or recovery-performance claims. Germanite is distinct from germanium element, a mixed ore feed and renierite; no microbial mechanism or inherited criticality designation is assigned. The Handbook's printed F-43n space-group label conflicts with P-43n in the primary refinement, so it is not silently treated as confirming that primary space-group assignment.

Kësterite

Kësterite is the mineral species Cu2ZnSnS4, recorded as grandfathered (G) with original-description year 1956 in the September 2026 IMA-CNMNC list. That year is not an IMA approval identifier. This natural mineral identity is distinct from elemental tin or zinc, mixed ores, synthetic CZTS photovoltaic materials, stannite and ferrokësterite. Historical papers often spell the name kesterite and describe Fe-bearing members. Černý and colleagues (2001) measured four natural Peerless pegmatite nodules, assigning two Zn-dominant specimens to ferroan kesterite from electron-microprobe composition and characterizing powder-diffraction unit cells. They explicitly named specimens by composition and did not refine cation ordering or establish each specimen's space group. Their specimens are compositionally variable natural materials, not pure endmembers. The microbial study below tested separately synthesized material identified by the historical authors as kesterite; it did not test natural kësterite, solar-cell films or industrial feed. No criticality designation is inherited from tin, zinc or copper.

Kutnohorite

Kutnohorite is the CaMn2+(CO3)2 mineral species listed by IMA-CNMNC in its September 2026 master list (PDF page 119). Its status is grandfathered; 1903 is the original-description year, not an IMA approval number. The Handbook of Mineralogy places it in the dolomite group and describes ordered calcium and manganese in approximately equal proportions, commonly with magnesium and ferrous-iron substitution. Its generalized formula Ca(Mn2+,Mg,Fe2+)(CO3)2 and natural-specimen analyses describe compositional variation around the IMA endmember, not an invariant manganese grade. The Handbook reports occurrence in deposits associated with manganese-bearing sediments and identifies analyzed material from Kutna Hora in the Czech Republic and Franklin, New Jersey. The spelling kutnahorite appears in a historical reference title; the accepted species name used here is kutnohorite. This mineral is distinct from the dolomite group as a whole, bulk manganese ore, elemental manganese and manganese oxalates. No microbial mechanism or criticality designation is assigned here.

Laforêtite

Laforêtite is the silver-indium sulfide mineral species AgInS2, listed as approved by IMA-CNMNC in September 2026 under 1995-006. Meisser and colleagues' original description concerns tiny natural inclusions in a single galena specimen collected from the Montgros mine dumps in Haute-Loire, France. Reflected-light observations, eight electron-microprobe analyses on two grains, and in-situ X-ray diffraction support the mineral assignment. The diffraction measurement used a roughly 20 by 30 micrometre inclusion; galena reflections were removed, and preferred orientation limits comparison of intensities with the separate synthetic reference. The natural material was assigned to the tetragonal low-temperature AgInS2 form. The authors' laboratory synthesis produced a multiphase comparator, not a pure natural specimen or microbial product; synthetic hardness measurements and preparation temperatures are not natural-specimen measurements. This species is distinct from elemental indium, bulk mine waste, and manufactured AgInS2 polymorphs considered solely by chemical formula. The sampled inclusions establish neither a bulk resource grade nor an extraction yield. No microbial mechanism, industrial recovery claim, or constituent-derived criticality designation is assigned.

Zircon

Zircon is the zirconium silicate mineral species Zr(SiO4), listed as grandfathered in the September 2026 IMA-CNMNC list. It is distinct from elemental zirconium, zirconia (ZrO2), zircon-bearing sand or concentrates, and other ZrSiO4 polymorphs. Hazen and Finger (1979) characterized two fragments of one natural, nonmetamict, gem-quality crystal from the Finsch Pipe kimberlite near Kimberley, South Africa, using single-crystal X-ray diffraction under pressure. Their specimen description reports nearly pure zircon with 1.2 weight percent HfO2 and 26 ppm uranium; these are specimen-specific values, not universal mineral composition or a resource grade. Measurements extended to 48.1 kbar; higher-pressure phase transitions discussed in that paper are predictions or references to other studies, not transformations observed in this experiment. The two fragments are not independent geological occurrences. This identity and structural evidence does not establish microbial transformation, recovery, industrial deployment or a criticality designation for the mineral.
