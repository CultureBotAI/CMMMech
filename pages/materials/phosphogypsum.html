Phosphogypsum secondary resource

A heterogeneous calcium-sulfate-rich residue generated during wet-process phosphoric acid manufacture from phosphate rock. It is distinct from natural gypsum mineral, phosphate-rock commodity and individual rare-earth elements. Curated laboratory evidence separates synthetic single-REE-doped model powders from an industrial residue sample. Neither experiment establishes industrial recovery, and constituent-element criticality is not inherited by the residue.

Microbial & Biochemical Mechanisms (2)

Gluconobacter spent-medium leaching of synthetic phosphogypsum models

bioleaching Microbial

Associated Organisms: Gluconobacter oxydans (NCBITaxon:442) [TaxonMech]

Spent medium from Gluconobacter oxydans NRRL B58 mobilized rare-earth elements from separately synthesized single-REE-doped calcium-sulfate powders. The tested agent was a microbial culture-derived lixiviant; the experiment does not demonstrate a requirement for direct living-cell contact. Organic-acid complexation and kinetic effects were proposed, without resolving the full lixiviant composition or a unique pathway.

Experimental Context & Analytical Limits:
The six separate synthetic feeds were doped with Y, Ce, Nd, Sm, Eu or Yb; they were not industrial phosphogypsum or a mixed-REE waste. Powders were synthesized from calcium/REE nitrate and sulfate/phosphate solutions, aged at 80 degrees C and dried at 90 degrees C; empirical hydration states in Table 1 were approximately 0.54-0.56 waters per formula unit, so a gypsum-dihydrate identity is not imposed on them. Section 2.2 describes glucose-grown B58 culture in a bioreactor by reference to prior methods; cell-removal preparation is not explicit in this paper. HPLC measured 220 mM gluconic acid and pH was 2.1. Triplicate leaching assays used 20 mg powder in 1 mL lixiviant at 25 degrees C for 24 h and 150 rpm. Filtered leachates were measured by ICP-OES; the repository manuscript prints a 0.22 mm filter unit, whose intended unit is not independently resolved here. Comparators were deionized water, commercial gluconic acid, sulfuric acid and phosphoric acid, with separate equal-pH and equal-molarity comparisons.
Curated Evidence Points:
Support Source: antonick2019
Sections 2.2-2.4 and 3.1-3.2, Table 2 and Figures 2-3 establish REE release into the lixiviant in these model assays. Reported percent-dissolved values were higher for spent medium than commercial gluconic acid, but the small Ce/Nd differences and overlapping uncertainty do not establish a uniformly significant advantage. Table 2 defines errors as 95% confidence intervals from triplicates. Mineral acids adjusted to pH 2.1 and water controls yielded no detectable REEs according to Section 3.1; numerical detection limits and individual null data were not supplied.
Partial Source: antonick2019
At equal 220 mM concentration, sulfuric acid generally performed better than spent medium; Y was an exception with broadly similar comparator behavior. Equal acid molarity does not mean matched pH. Table 2's Ce leachate concentration and percent-dissolved ordering differ between the two organic lixiviants, limiting a simple concentration-based superiority claim. Section 4.3 explicitly warns that approximately 1 wt% REE synthetic feeds differ from more dilute, mixed industrial residues. Thermodynamic simulations omit kinetic and mass-transfer effects and do not prove the proposed mechanism. Dissolved fractions are not separated products or whole-process yields.

Gluconobacter culture-associated release from Xifeng phosphogypsum

bioleaching Microbial

Associated Organisms: Gluconobacter oxydans (NCBITaxon:442) [TaxonMech]

A supplier-labelled Gluconobacter oxydans culture was associated with rare-earth release and declining pH during treatment of an industrial phosphogypsum sample. Source inconsistencies and unmatched controls limit this to qualitative culture-associated mobilization; no accepted extraction percentage or demonstrated microbial advantage is assigned.

Experimental Context & Analytical Limits:
Zhang et al. used Xifeng, Guizhou material ground through 200 mesh and dried. No strain accession or independent taxonomic authentication was supplied. Culture medium started at pH 6.8; Table 4 reports glucose, calcium carbonate and yeast extract at 0.1, 0.02 and 0.01 g/L. The protocol describes 250 mL flasks, 28 degrees C, 150 rpm and nutrient addition in 100 mL then 50 mL stages. Exact timing and liquid/solid accounting are unclear. Liquid-to-solid ratios 2:1, 4:1, 8:1 and 16:1 were compared; the ratio's units and handling of changing volume were not explicit. Samples were centrifuged and supernatants measured by ICP-MS. Culture removal before contact was not reported; this is not assumed to be a cell-free lixiviant assay.
Curated Evidence Points:
Support Source: zhang2025
Sections 2.3 and 3.2-3.3 with Figures 5-7 report increasing supernatant REE signals with declining pH in culture-containing laboratory flasks. Table 1 and XRD Figure 2 support a mixed calcium-sulfate-rich feed; individual REE host phases were not established. The observation does not itself identify the chemical form of released REEs or an isolated recovered product.
Partial Source: zhang2025
Figure 7 labels the release axis in micrograms/kg while Section 2.3.3 reports micrograms/L, and its plotted maximum is around day 24 rather than the conclusion's approximately day 21. Table 2's feed inventory, the stated liquid-to-solid treatment and the reported 24.67% total extraction lack a reproducible common denominator. The pH experiment compares inoculated acidic arms with an uninoculated pH 6.8 control, without a matched acidified sterile control. Figure error bars lack a stated replicate number or error definition in the inspected article. Organic-acid identities and concentrations were not measured here. Neither the 24.67% total nor 55.67% Nd claim is accepted as a verified recovery yield.
