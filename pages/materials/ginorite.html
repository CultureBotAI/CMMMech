Ginorite mineral

Ginorite is the grandfathered calcium borate species listed as Ca2B14O20(OH)6(H2O)3·2H2O in the September 2026 IMA-CNMNC list. Its 1934 entry is a description year, not an approval identifier. The older Ca2[B14O20(OH)6]·5H2O formula has the same elemental stoichiometry. A natural Sasso Pisano crystal studied by Perchiazzi et al. has a monoclinic P21/a structure with borate sheets, calcium sites and hydrogen bonding; three water molecules per formula unit coordinate calcium and two do not. Hydrogen positions were refined with O-H distance restraints. The study's tabulated chemical analyses came from earlier publications, while its own preliminary SEM/EDAX screen found strontium below detection. This species is distinct from strontioginorite, elemental boron and the borates commodity. Neither a criticality designation nor microbial extraction or commercial recovery is inferred from its boron content.

Laboratory endothermic response on heating

other

Natural ginorite from Twenty Mule Team Canyon, Death Valley, showed an initial endothermic response at 70-80 degrees C and reported troughs at 100, 150, 310, 360 and 470 degrees C during differential thermal analysis. These are instrumental thermal features of that specimen, not identified product phases, an equilibrium decomposition sequence or a boron recovery yield.

Experimental Context & Analytical Limits:
Allen's 1957 procedure started with a cold furnace and used a nominal heating rate of 30 degrees C per minute through 500 degrees C. Samples were sieved to the reported -50+200 mesh range and lightly tamped in crucibles; ginorite was run at medium sensitivity. Temperature readings were taken at 10-degree intervals. Each mineral was analyzed at least three times, but individual ginorite replicate curves and statistical uncertainties were not reported. The mineral was checked microscopically, chemically and by X-ray diffraction before the thermal experiment. Sample mass, controlled atmosphere, evolved-gas analysis and post-run ginorite phase identification were not reported.

Methods on pp. 194-195 and repeat-run statements on p. 196 constrain the instrumental context. The alumina-mixing and post-heating diffraction tests described for other named borates on p. 195 do not include ginorite and are not transferred to this arm.
Context_only Source: allen_kramer1957
The companion study documents Death Valley ginorite associated with sassolite and clay, purified by warm water dissolution of sassolite and drying at 105 degrees C. Table 1 retains an insoluble remainder and separates original from renormalized chemistry. The thermal report cites this characterization but does not establish that every thermal aliquot underwent the same drying protocol. Neither source measures a ginorite extraction efficiency or an isolated boron product.
