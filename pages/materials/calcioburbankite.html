Calcioburbankite mineral

A natural REE-bearing carbonate mineral, distinct from burbankite and the burbankite group. The September 2026 IMA list gives Na3(Ca,Ce,Sr,La)3(CO3)5 and approval 1993-001. The 1995 original description concerns a Mont Saint-Hilaire holotype, Canadian Museum of Nature catalogue 50804; its precise quarry and geological setting were uncertain. Its surface was partly altered, and a separately encountered La-rich specimen was not pooled with the holotype. Three analyses on three grains support Ca-dominant chemistry. Carbon was detected separately, but the CO2 amount was calculated stoichiometrically, not independently measured. Infrared analysis did not detect OH or water. Powder and precession diffraction established hexagonal symmetry but left three possible space groups; the 1995 paper did not resolve an atomic structure. It cautions that diffraction alone is insufficient to distinguish related species. Table 2 contains inconsistent mean/range entries, including Pr2O3, and a Ca atomic-proportion range apparently duplicated from Na; no disputed range is adopted. Rapid dissolution in 10 percent HCl was a qualitative identification observation without reported time, temperature, loading, yield or isolated product. It is not curated as a recovery mechanism. The later 2001 structure study remains outside this record's accepted evidence. A 2024 hydrothermal diamond-anvil experiment supports a separate, qualified observation of abiotic crystallization of an author-assigned calcioburbankite phase from a carbonate brine-melt; it does not establish a pure recovered product or a run-specific REE yield. No criticality designation, microbial activity or industrial deployment is asserted.

Microbial & Biochemical Mechanisms (1)

Abiotic crystallization of an author-assigned calcioburbankite phase

other

In run 8, a charge containing natural bastnaesite, Na2CO3, water and calcite became homogeneous at 690 degrees C and an estimated 9.5 kbar. On cooling, the authors observed calcite at 660 degrees C, followed by needle-like crystals assigned to calcioburbankite at 635 degrees C (Figure 4 panel p8, 8.0 kbar). This is a laboratory crystallization observation in a mixed mineral assemblage, not an extraction or isolated-product yield.

Experimental Context & Analytical Limits:
A gold-lined rhenium gasket in a hydrothermal diamond-anvil cell held the charge; the natural bastnaesite was from Maoniuping, Sichuan. Mineralization runs were heated at 10 degrees C/min and cooled at 5–10 degrees C/min. Run-8 pressures were estimated using the calcite Raman peak at 1086 inverse centimetres. Exact run-wise starting masses, water volume, replicate counts and final quantitative phase fractions were not reported. Run-8 REE concentration was explicitly not determined because carbonate contributions from carbocernaite and nyerereite could not be separated. Post-run samples were washed with two to three drops of deionized water to remove Na2CO3 hydrates and other soluble surface salts, then examined by SEM-EDS; the washed assemblage is not a complete product inventory. Representative EDS counts and Raman spectra support the authors' phase assignment without establishing a quantitative Ca/REE composition, a refined crystal structure or product purity.

Context_only Source: yuan2024
Supplementary Text p.2 and Figure S2 p.9 describe a separate lower-pressure, melt–fluid immiscibility experiment with bastnaesite, Na2CO3, water and calcite. The run-S2 panel labels show 675 degrees C/3.8 kbar before cooling and calcioburbankite-labelled crystals at 620 degrees C/3.3 kbar. Its post-run BSE/EDS assemblage contains author-assigned calcioburbankite, nyerereite and villiaumite; Figure S2 contains no Raman panel. S2 is not treated as a run-8 replicate or as a quantitative recovery balance. The paper's wider geological interpretations are not industrial-process or microbial evidence.
