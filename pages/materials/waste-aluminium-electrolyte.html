Waste aluminium electrolyte secondary resource

Discarded fluoride-rich aluminium-smelting electrolyte investigated as a lithium-bearing secondary resource. Kordloo et al. describe this material in the context of Hall-Heroult aluminium production and report a waste electrolyte specimen containing Na, Al, Ca, K, Li, Mg and F. The particular supplier, plant, batch history and sampling design are not identified. This mixed process residue is distinct from spent potlining, secondary aluminium dross, aluminium salt slag and spent battery electrolyte; it is not a pure lithium mineral or an isolated lithium product.

Microbial & Biochemical Mechanisms (1)

Lithium dissolution in fungal and bacterial culture systems

bioleaching Microbial

Lithium was measured in filtered liquid during laboratory treatment of waste aluminium electrolyte in Aspergillus niger and Glutamicibacter nicotianae culture systems. The study supports culture-associated dissolution under its reported conditions. Different media and starting pH, with no reported matched sterile or pH-controlled abiotic arms, prevent attribution of the difference between systems to organism identity alone. Acid formation, amino-acid complexation and later precipitation are proposed causes, rather than separately established lithium-specific reactions.

Experimental Context & Analytical Limits:
Sections 2.1-2.3, manuscript pages 6-9, and Tables 1-2 describe jaw crushing, pulverization and homogenization to d80 47.5 micrometres; feed Li is reported as 1.13 wt.%. The fungal culture came from the Soil and Water Research Institute collection in Karaj without an accession. The bacterium was isolated from Choqart iron-mine soil/wastewater/tailings samples and identified here by morphology with a prior-paper citation; no experimental strain accession is given. Each 250 mL flask held 90 mL medium plus 10 mL inoculum at 30 degrees C and 150 rpm for up to 30 days, with 10, 30 or 50 g/L nominal pulp density. Bacterial LB medium contained 10 g/L NaCl, 5 g/L yeast extract and 10 g/L tryptone, initially pH 7; fungal modified Sperber medium contained 11 g/L glucose, 0.5 g/L yeast extract and 0.25 g/L MgSO4.7H2O, initially pH 3.2. Inoculum density, independent replicate count, error-bar definition, sampling-volume correction and a complete lithium balance are unresolved. Filtrate lithium was measured by ICP-OES. No isolated lithium product or industrial operation was demonstrated.
Curated Evidence Points:
Support Source: kordloo2025_si
Table S2 reports nondecreasing dissolved-lithium percentages through day 22, followed by lower values at day 30 in all six arms. At 10 g/L the fungal arm is listed as 100.00% at day 22 and 86.10% at day 30; the bacterial arm is 43.75% and 37.50%, respectively. These are the source's dissolution percentages, not independently validated recoveries or isolated-product yields. Table S2 places the first two nonzero samples at days 10 and 15, whereas main Figures 1c and 2b plot them at about days 5 and 10. The sequences must not be silently aligned for a kinetic fit.
Partial Source: kordloo2025_aam
Section 3.1/Figures 1-2 supports the observed contrasting pH trajectories and the day-22 maximum followed by a decline. The abstract and conclusion instead assign the bacterial 43.75% maximum to 30 days. Equation 1 on manuscript page 9 prints dissolution as V*C1/(m*C0)*100, with both C0 and C1 in mg/L, V in mL and m in g; the solid-feed concentration basis and conversion needed for a dimensionless percentage are unresolved. The printed percentages are therefore retained as reported values without recalibrating the assay or asserting total lithium recovery. Visible error bars do not establish their definition or the number of independent experiments.
Partial Source: kordloo2025_si
Table S1 prints amino-acid and organic-acid concentrations before and after treatment in mg/g, without a resolved mass denominator. All four listed fungal organic acids decrease between the two columns; the amino-acid changes have mixed signs. Some organic acid entries exceed 1000 mg/g. Neither these values nor the nutrient-containing media establish net microbial metabolite production without an appropriate blank and clarified units. They provide contextual chemical observations, not proof that a particular ligand caused lithium release.
Partial Source: kordloo2025_aam
Section 3.3/Figures 3-5 assigns fluorite, cryolite, simmonsite and elpasolite in the feed/residues and an additional prosopite phase in fungal residues. SEM-EDX percentages describe selected local fields and do not report lithium or close a bulk lithium balance. These observations do not identify a lithium-bearing precipitate responsible for the late decline. FTIR text and plotted spectra disagree about some sample-to-band assignments. Section 3.4/ Figure 6 gives fitted diffusion/reaction expressions, including an unexplained 0.45 factor in Equation 3, over the rising portion of the curves. The unresolved sample-time correspondence, excluded late decline and model assumptions prevent treating these fits as independent proof of a rate-limiting mechanism.
