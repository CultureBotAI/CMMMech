## Calcium-oxalate source assessment

The primary is Frank-Kamenetskaya, Ivanyuk, Zelenskaya, Izatulina, Kalashnikov, Vlasov and Polyanskaya, *Minerals* 9(11):656 (2019). It reports field mineral occurrence and a separate fungal exposure. These are two contexts in one study, not independent replication of a common mechanism. Table 1 is a literature compilation; its species/rock associations were not imported as new experimental evidence.

### Species identity and provenance

IMA September 2026 page 233 lists weddellite as Ca(C2O4)(H2O)2, grandfathered, with original-description year 1942. Page 234 lists whewellite as Ca(C2O4)(H2O), approved, with literal notation 1967 s.p. Neither year was converted into a new-mineral accession. Handbook entries distinguish tetragonal weddellite from monoclinic whewellite. The primary's variable-water description of weddellite is not a measured occupancy of these specimens. The Handbook reports potential atmospheric dehydration from weddellite to whewellite; no assumption is made that it occurred in these exact samples.

Official NCBI taxonomy 5061 is *Aspergillus niger* at species rank. The original's KF768341 corresponds to KF768341.1, labelled strain Ch 4/07 with isolation source `grecian marble`. This supports the strain-name provenance in the paper, not independent authentication of its experimental culture or a complete genome. Field lichens retain source names without invented species IDs, strain accessions or causality assigned to each community member.

### Field-associated phase evidence

Methods 2.1 and Results 3.1–3.3 describe samples collected in 2018 from Khibiny ore-block dumps and the Kola Science Centre Rock Garden. The rock is mainly fluorapatite plus nepheline with minor aegirine, titanite and titanomagnetite. Field crystals occurred in thalli assigned to *Farnoldia jurana* (sample 1), *Lecanora polytropa* and *Polysporina simplex* (sample 2), at fluorapatite-rich areas. Table 2 documents other lichens and micromycetes in the biofilms, not isolated producers of the oxalate.

Figure 3 trace 2 is explicitly the *F. jurana* specimen and contains source-assigned whewellite/weddellite with substrate peaks. Separate diffraction traces for each other lichen are not displayed. Figures 4b and 6 support elemental/morphological context and crystal–hypha association. The authors' phrase about close phase proportions is not a quantitative refinement or mass fraction. Local formation pH, temperature, age and metabolic state were not measured. Oxalate secretion by each mycobiont and transfer of calcium from one host phase were not directly tested.

The proposed roles of Sr/Al impurities, citrate and the complex microbial community are hypotheses. EDX of carbon-coated specimens cannot alone establish the hydrate, ion valence or lattice-site substitution. The field material supplies no matched sterile comparator, controlled community perturbation, metabolite series or extraction mass balance. These limits are retained rather than treating the field observation as a proven recovery process.

### Culture-associated whewellite and contrasting weddellite outcome

Methods 2.2 used *A. niger* Ch4/07 conidia from a 10-day solid culture, approximately 5 by 5 mm biofilm-free fragments from the sample-1 block, 15 mL Capek-Dox medium, 20–25 degrees C and 2–15 days in triplicate. The source supplies medium ingredients and initial pH 5.5. Results 3.4 give pH 4.0 on day 5 and 4.5 at the endpoint. Mineral mass, inoculum density, sterilization and post-harvest hydration control are unspecified. Biofilm-free starting rock is not an uninoculated incubation control; no such matched outcome or killed-cell arm is supplied.

Figure 3 trace 3 shows day-5 whewellite in a mixed substrate/product sample. Figure 7 shows day-5 and day-12 surface products. The text reports whewellite in all three replicates; it does not report replicated quantitative phase fractions, isolated product mass or formation rates. Fungal acid action is plausible but acid species and calcium sources were not directly resolved here.

The culture products were reported as whewellite only, whereas field material contained both hydrates. This is a useful conditional counterexample to assuming that the same laboratory exposure produced weddellite. It is not an exact-zero detection claim, a universal inability of *A. niger* to form weddellite, or proof that field community complexity alone determines phase selection. The separately cited marble study uses a different substrate and is not a matched control in the 2019 experiment. Handling-induced dehydration remains a possible confounder, not an asserted transformation.

## Antigorite and amphibole limits

The Liu 2022 study is a strong potential antigorite experiment, but abstract mentions of Streptomyces FXJ8.102, etched surfaces and glushinskite cannot establish its preparation, biomass state, controls, quantitative chemistry or phase evidence. The acquired API object is metadata, not full text. No antigorite record or history was created from these excerpts.

The retained original Vigliaturo 2018 EPMA Table 1 and Figure 3 state Mg/(Mg+Fe) 0.86–0.90 and place the Passo di Caldenno amphibole at the actinolite–tremolite boundary. The 2020 preparation study describes hand selection, wet grinding and filtration of the source rock. These preparations do not prove two separately assayed pure mineral species. A new primary-resource owner for one laboratory specimen would also need a useful, defensible resource boundary rather than merely relabelling a prepared mineral batch. No such owner was added. The prior Choi supplement limitation remains a separate unresolved task.
