Manganese ore primary resource

Manganese ore here denotes natural manganese-bearing geological feed, including the mixed ore excavated from an abandoned Michigan mine in the curated study. It is a primary resource, distinct from the pyrolusite mineral species, elemental manganese, electrolytic manganese products and processing residues. The source calls its sample pyrolusite ore, but reports several manganese minerals. This record does not define a traded product grade or inherit constituent criticality.

Microbial & Biochemical Mechanisms (2)

Manganese-ore leaching with biologically mobilized iron

bioleaching Microbial

A mixed-culture iron-leaching stage generated Fe(II)-bearing liquor used to mobilize manganese from a separate mixed-ore bed. Laboratory results support manganese release in this biologically supplied reductant system. The authors report carbonate suppression relative to prior single-stage work, but conflicting figure annotations leave that comparison unresolved. The study does not establish a sterile chemical second stage, zero carbonate formation, or commercial recovery performance.

Experimental Context & Analytical Limits:
Living mixed metal-reducing cultures originated from a fen in Houghton County, Michigan, and previously adapted cultures inoculated the iron stage. No individual strain or causal community member is resolved here. Fermented Typha latifolia biomass plus 5 g/L sucrose supplied organic nutrient liquid. Methods 2.4 and Figure 5 describe two 250 mL Buechner flasks in series: the drawing labels 200 g iron feed and 350 g manganese ore. Methods 2.1 identifies the iron feed as tailings from an unnamed iron-ore production facility, smaller than 0.15 mm; the manganese ore came from an abandoned Keweenaw County mine and was crushed below 3 mm. After seven days of adaptation, 300 mL fresh nutrient per week was supplied in five 60 mL increments. Packed beds, fermentation locks and a submerged manganese-flask outlet were used to maintain anaerobic conditions. Experiments were run in triplicate. A numerical flask incubation temperature is not specified in these methods; Section 3.6 discusses ambient-temperature operation. Reported operating pH was 4.7 +/- 0.7 in the iron stage and 4.4 +/- 0.8 in the manganese stage. Overflow Mn was measured by colorimetric periodate oxidation and Fe(II) by 1,10-phenanthroline spectrophotometry; filtration and operational dissolved-fraction details are not supplied. These assays do not resolve the mineral phase contributing each metal.

Sections 3.1–3.3 compare the two-stage experiment with single-stage biotic and abiotic experiments from earlier studies, references 7 and 13. They are historical comparisons, not matched contemporaneous two-stage controls. The paper does not provide a sterile two-stage or matched chemical-Fe(II) comparator that isolates microbial necessity or the relative acid and iron contributions. Section 3.6 explicitly allows microbial carryover into the manganese stage and states that dominant organisms and relative activities were not assessed. Thus the second stage is not established as cell-free, and no taxon-specific or mineral-specific causal claim is made.

Section 3.3 states that two-stage residue lacks manganese-carbonate peaks, in contrast with prior single-stage work. Figure 11, however, places black rhodochrosite-labelled markers above both traces, including approximately 24.2 and 31.4 degrees in the upper two-stage trace. The text and figure annotations therefore conflict. This record does not decide whether those markers indicate confirmed carbonate detection or misplaced annotations; raw diffraction data or an author clarification would be needed. Without a reported detection limit or carbon balance, neither complete carbonate absence nor cessation of microbial CO2 production is established. Iron-mediated reduction and carbonate suppression remain incompletely isolated explanations.
Partial Source: borkar2026
Methods 2.5 and Table 6 describe 19 L fermentation, iron and manganese units, with 20 kg ore charges in the leaching tanks, flow of 5 L/day and downstream 12 L oxidation/settling tanks. Figure 12 labels Mn at 767 mg/L leaving manganese leaching and 98 mg/L after the iron stage of the recirculating flow; 98 mg/L is not the immediate oxidation-tank effluent, which is labelled 152 mg/L. The text's calculated 3.35 g/day available for precipitation is therefore not accepted as a directly weighed daily recovery or a closed full-process mass balance.
Support Source: borkar2026
Section 3.5 describes downstream solids dried at 100 degrees C for 24 hours, washed and roasted at 750 degrees C in an oxidizing atmosphere. Figure 13 assigns the main roasted phase to Mn3O4. Table 7 reports 93 wt% Mn3O4 on an oxide-equivalent basis converted from acid-digest ICP elemental analysis, as its footnote specifies. It is not a direct quantitative XRD phase-purity measurement, battery qualification, or electrolytic MnO2 product. Absence of carbonate peaks after roasting does not establish absence before heating.
Partial Source: borkar2026
Section 3.6 explicitly identifies the downstream processing as laboratory-scale characterization, with optimization, scale-up, economics and long-term industrial stability left for future work. The 19 L apparatus is bench-scale despite the pilot terminology. This and the iron-ore-tailings record represent the same linked experiment.

Fungal transformation of mixed Nkamouna manganiferous laterite

biomineralization Microbial

Associated Organisms: Aspergillus niger (NCBITaxon:5061) [TaxonMech]

Living Aspergillus niger ATCC 1015 cultures were associated with surface-crust formation and a source-assigned manganese oxalate dihydrate phase in recovered debris from natural cobalt-bearing Nkamouna laterite. Residual source minerals and untransformed particles remained. Qualitative elemental signals associate cobalt and nickel with crusts, but do not establish crystallographic substitution, selective extraction from one host phase, oxidation-state changes or isolated-metal recovery.

Experimental Context & Analytical Limits:
Ferrier et al. used natural laterite obtained directly from Nkamouna, Cameroon, supplied by D. S. Mulroy, and broken into fragments and powder with a mortar and pestle. Powder was sieved below 90 micrometers; the mineral preparation was oven-sterilized at 105 degrees C for at least 24 hours. Sterile powder was mixed into molten malt extract agar at approximately 60 degrees C, at 0.2% w/v, with approximately 25 mL per 90-mm plate. Separate fragments were placed in wells in agar; their dimensions and mass per plate are not specified. Plates received a 5-mm plug from the growing edge of an A. niger ATCC 1015 colony and were incubated for 14 days at 25 degrees C in darkness. NCBITaxon:5061 identifies the species; the reported experimental strain is separately grounded by the ATCC catalogue, without verifying the experimental culture by sequencing. Matched uninoculated mineral plates and mineral-free fungal and abiotic plates were included. After biomass removal and surface-pH measurement, powder debris was washed from agar in water at approximately 70 degrees C, allowed to settle, washed twice again, and dried in a desiccator for at least 48 hours. The recovered dry-phase identity does not establish its hydration state in the living culture. These were laboratory plate assays, not an industrial process.

Curated Evidence Points:
Support Source: ferrier2022
Introduction and Minerals methods, Table 1 and Figure 5A: the mixed natural feed contains source-assigned lithiophorite and cryptomelane. Partial XRF composition is reported on the table's mixed oxide/element percentage basis, including 30.91% MnO, 27.56% Fe2O3, 16.24% Al2O3, 5.04% Co and 2.87% Ni; MnO is an oxide-equivalent assay entry, not proof of an MnO mineral phase. No mineral-specific metal inventory or assay uncertainty is supplied. This feed falls within the existing geological manganese-ore resource scope, rather than defining a pure mineral, product grade or separate cobalt element record.
Support Source: ferrier2022
Figures 1 and 3 show crusts on inoculated fragments and powder relative to uninoculated controls. Figures 2B and 4B show Mn, Co and Ni signals associated with sampled crusts. Figure 5B, compared with 5A, is assigned by the authors to residual lithiophorite and cryptomelane plus manganese oxalate dihydrate (lindbergite). Results explicitly state that powder transformation was incomplete and many particles showed no morphological change. SEM/EDXA and bulk recovered-powder XRPD support this narrow association, not quantitative phase fractions, a pure product or equal attack on each source mineral.
Partial Source: ferrier2022
Results and Discussion describe fragment crusts as lacking Al and containing Mg, but the original Figure 2B retains an Al label and displays no labelled Mg peak. Those stronger elemental absence/presence claims remain unresolved. Figure 5 explicitly concerns recovered powder; it is not a separately displayed fragment-crust diffraction result, despite broader Discussion wording. Powder Figure 4B also retains Al, and Ca-rich bipyramidal structures in Figure 4C are inferred as weddellite by morphology/composition without a separately displayed diffraction identification. None of these observations is used to assert complete Al/Fe removal or confirmed Mg incorporation.
Support Source: ferrier2022
Table 2, not the Table 1 cross-reference in Results, reports 14-day agar-surface pH beneath laterite-grown biomass of 4.09 +/- 0.29 (mean +/- SD, n=5), versus 5.92 +/- 0.04 in uninoculated laterite plates (n=3). Fungal mineral-free controls were 2.85 +/- 0.06 (n=4). The reported 97.7% tolerance index is a dry fungal-biomass ratio to mineral-free growth, and the 1.42 growth-rate ratio uses colony expansion on days 2–5; neither quantity measures ore transformation or metal recovery.
Partial Source: ferrier2022
Experimental procedures and Discussion provide no pH-matched acid-only, purified-oxalate, killed-biomass or cell-free-metabolite comparison for the Nkamouna experiment. Oxalate-mediated dissolution/precipitation is an interpretation supported by product assignment and the organism's known physiology, not a directly quantified secretion or exclusive molecular pathway. Co(III)-to-Co(II) reduction is proposed without oxidation-state spectroscopy; EDXA does not establish valence or distinguish lattice incorporation from associated phases. Dissolved-metal, residual-feed and recovered-product mass balances are absent. Figure 5 gives typical patterns from several determinations without resolving independent culture versus analytical replication; heating, washing and drying may affect recovered phases.
Context_only Source: ferrier2022
Figures 6–8 and the separate synthetic Co-birnessite arm are not measurements of natural laterite. Their complete-transformation language and pooled diffraction subsample cannot be transferred to the incomplete Nkamouna result. Figure 8's heading says Nkamouna laterite, while its detailed caption and methods identify synthetic Co-birnessite. The Introduction's attribution of a Mn(III) oxalate intermediate to Wei2012 is not accepted as a measurement in this experiment. No criticality designation or industrial performance is inferred.
