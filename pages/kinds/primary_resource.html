A geological rare-earth-bearing feed represented by the REE-concentrated crushed allanite ore supplied as Zappall from WRE in Schmitz et al. (2025). This concentrated primary resource is distinct from an ideal mineral species, elemental rare earths, their commodities and secondary waste. No constituent criticality designation is inherited.

Basaltic rock

Naturally occurring basaltic geological material, represented here by six heterogeneous drill-core specimens from ICDP HSDP2, Hilo, Hawaii: basaltic flows, basaltic hyaloclastites and pillow basalts. This resource is not a single mineral species, pure basaltic glass, an elemental metal or a recovered commodity. The specimens contain different proportions of glass, primary silicates and secondary alteration phases. The source's manufactured BAS glasses are experimental analogues and are not the natural feed represented by this record. No constituent criticality designation is inherited.

Carbonatite-associated rare-earth ore

Naturally occurring, mixed rare-earth-bearing ore associated with alkaline-carbonatite deposits, anchored here to the tested Mushgia Khudag material from Mongolia. The deposit association does not establish that every sampled rock meets a modal carbonatite classification. The curated feed contains quartz, apatite, calcite and other phases, with monazite and parisite assignments; it is neither a pure mineral nor a processed waste. Laboratory culture pretreatment followed by sulfuric-acid leaching is represented as a treatment-associated observation with unresolved causal controls. Neither industrial deployment nor inherited criticality is asserted.

Cassiterite-polymetallic sulfide ore

A heterogeneous natural tin-bearing geological feed containing cassiterite and sulfide minerals, represented here by the Myanmar ore studied by Wang et al. (2025). The crushed experimental ore contained source-assigned pyrite, cassiterite, quartz, tourmaline and mica. Its mine and deposit are not specified. Crushing does not establish a processing-waste origin. This resource is distinct from cassiterite or pyrrhotite mineral species, elemental tin, a tin commodity and a produced concentrate. The study does not establish extraction from a pure mineral or properties of all ores described by this name. No criticality designation is inherited from tin.

Chalcopyrite-enargite concentrate

Mixed copper-sulfide mineral concentrates represented by the D3, Eb and Ea feeds in Oyama et al. (2021), distinct from the constituent chalcopyrite and enargite mineral species, the nearly pure model chalcopyrite tested separately in that paper, and the DC-I/DC-II/DC-III enargite-pyrite concentrates examined in Okibe et al. The displayed Ea phase labels total 97%, so the figure is not treated as a closed compositional balance. Mine, batch and beneficiation details are not established; the acknowledgments identify an academic supplier for Ea. This owner denotes concentrated geological feed, not a documented discarded residue, a pure phase or a traded product specification. Cobalt-rich ferromanganese crusts

Naturally occurring layered iron-manganese oxide and oxyhydroxide deposits on exposed marine rock surfaces, commonly seamounts. This resource identity covers the cobalt-bearing, predominantly hydrogenetic crusts formed by accumulation from seawater; it does not denote every hydrothermal encrustation. The heterogeneous crust material is distinct from sediment-hosted polymetallic nodules, constituent cobalt or manganese elements, a pure mineral species, and a processing residue. Maciąg et al. characterize crusts and their altered basalt interfaces from Dirck Hartog Ridge; their sample-specific mineral mixtures are not a universal formula or grade. Collection and physical preparation do not make this geological resource secondary. The laboratory mechanism below records conditional redistribution of cobalt and nickel in whole-crust microcosms; it does not establish a specific receiving phase, a purified metal product or industrial deployment. The resource inherits no constituent criticality.

Dunite

Dunite is an ultramafic rock and geological feed, not a mineral species or elemental magnesium. BGS Rock Classification Scheme code DUN defines this peridotite type by more than 90% olivine in the assemblage of olivine, pyroxene and hornblende. The curated experiments used three powders from a North Carolina dunite deposit, including strongly altered material with much lower remaining olivine fractions. Their laboratory results do not describe every dunite, a pure forsterite feed, a waste tailing, or a traded product grade. No criticality designation is inherited from constituent elements.

Enargite-pyrite concentrate

Mixed high-arsenic mineral concentrates represented by DC-I, DC-II and DC-III in Okibe et al. (2022), distinct from pure enargite or pyrite and from elemental copper or arsenic. Company-supplied mineral liberation analyses assign enargite and pyrite, respectively, as 51.8 and 34.6 wt% in DC-I, 37.4 and 47.3 wt% in DC-II, and 26.1 and 63.1 wt% in DC-III. Original XRD also identifies these phases, with a source-labelled tennantite contribution in DC-III. Other copper minerals occur, so dissolved copper cannot be assigned exclusively to enargite. DC-I and DC-III were supplied by Sumitomo Metal Mining and DC-II by JX Nippon Mining and Metals; mine, batch and detailed beneficiation provenance are not reported. The paper frames dirty concentrates as flotation waste separated from clean smelter feed. This record denotes the characterized concentrated geological feeds without asserting prior disposal, a tailings stockpile or a traded product specification. No mineral-species identifier or constituent criticality designation is transferred to the mixed resource.

Eucrite meteorite

Natural basaltic achondritic meteorite material considered as an experimental feed, distinct from an L-chondrite, terrestrial basalt, a pure mineral or secondary resource. The curated example is the EU specimen in Tonietti et al. (2026). Its reported mineral assemblage is dominated by plagioclase and pyroxene, with minor olivine and quartz. The paper attributes eucrite material to Vesta but gives no exact meteorite name or accession for EU; Table 1 says Skylab (US), whereas the acknowledgments thank SkyFall Meteorites. The experimental specimen's provenance is therefore incompletely resolved. No resource-level criticality designation is inferred from its constituent trace metals.

Eudialyte concentrate

A beneficiated geological feed represented by the TANBREEZ concentrate from southern Greenland characterized by Ma and colleagues (2019). The source describes crushing followed by partly magnetic selection; this is an upgraded mixed mineral concentrate, distinct from the uncharacterized crushed eudialyte ore in Hunter's 2020 thesis, a formal eudialyte mineral species or group, elemental zirconium and recovered rare-earth products. Table I reports 1.60 wt% total rare earth elements and 6.46 wt% Zr in the laboratory feed. QEMSCAN assigns 67.05% of the material to eudialyte, alongside amphibole, nepheline, feldspar and other phases; this classification does not establish a pure species or a universal commercial concentrate specification. The source acknowledges the Geological Survey of Finland as concentrate supplier. No criticality designation is inherited from constituent elements.

Natural kaolin feed

Naturally occurring kaolinite-bearing clay feed, represented here by a mixed ore from Bilecik-Söğüt, Turkey. XRD identified kaolinite, quartz and microcline as major phases. This resource is distinct from the mineral species kaolinite, discarded kaolin mine waste and a refined alumina or traded purity-defined product. Table 1 reports 0.82 wt% Fe2O3, 21.79 wt% Al2O3 and 66.83 wt% SiO2 as bulk XRF oxide equivalents, not mineral fractions or proof of a particular iron phase. The record does not inherit a criticality designation from aluminium or another constituent.

L-chondrite meteorite

Naturally occurring L-group ordinary chondritic meteorite material considered as a potential experimental feed, not a mineral species, secondary waste or established commercial ore. The curated experiment used fragments identified by its authors as Northwest Africa 869, an L3-6 regolith breccia, containing heterogeneous silicate, sulfide and metal phases. These Earth-collected fragments were transported to the International Space Station; the study did not mine an asteroid in situ. Results apply to the tested specimens and conditions, not every L-chondrite. Constituent metals do not confer a criticality designation on this resource.

Lithium-mica ore

Natural lithium-bearing ore containing lithium micas together with other mineral phases. The 2020 curated experiment used crushed ore from Beauvoir, France, supplied by the University of Birmingham and called lepidolite by Sedlakova-Kadukova et al. Its initial XRD pattern was assigned to polylithionite, muscovite and kaolinite in supplementary Figure S2A. These are source-qualified assignments in a heterogeneous feed, not a certified pure polylithionite specimen or quantitative phase proportions. Lepidolite is series terminology, not an unambiguous mineral-species identifier. This extracted geological feed remains a primary resource, distinct from an individual mica species, elemental lithium, a processing waste or a purified lithium product. A separate 2025 experiment used a supplier-provided mixed feed called lepidolite, with mica, albite and quartz assignments and additional celadonite labels in its initial diffraction figure; no mine provenance or quantitative species composition was supplied. The two feeds are separate experiments within this resource category. No criticality designation or industrial deployment is inferred from its lithium content.

Ludwigite boron-iron ore

Ludwigite ore denotes a heterogeneous boron-bearing iron ore or its beneficiated boron-iron concentrate, represented here by the mixed geological feed used by Liang et al. (2024). It is not the mineral species ludwigite, elemental boron or iron, a borates commodity specification, or the secondary residue left after processing. The experimental substrate for water leaching was a soda-ash-roasted derivative of that concentrate; its altered composition must not be assigned to the original ore. The paper's Table 2 reports 5.12 wt% B2O3 and 58.23 wt% total iron in this reduced feed, and Figure 9 identifies metallic iron, sodium magnesium silicate, sodium silicate and magnesium-ferrous oxide. Its bulk-feed result cannot establish which individual original boron mineral supplied the dissolved boron. The exact mine/sample identifier and original batch mineral proportions are not established in the inspected 2024 article, which refers to earlier work for the ore characterization. No constituent's criticality designation is transferred to this resource.

Monazite ore

A heterogeneous geological rare-earth phosphate feed represented by the high-grade monazite ore supplied by Lynas Rare Earths, Australia, to van Alin et al. (2023). The reviewed study does not establish its precise mine or deposit of origin. XRD, TIMA and elemental analyses characterize a mixed feed rather than a pure monazite species: the supplement reports monazite-group and other phosphate phases with additional gangue. This resource is distinct from monazite-(Ce), monazite-(Nd), elemental rare earths, a rare-earth commodity and secondary processing waste. The source's high-grade description is not an independently certified commercial grade. No constituent criticality designation is inherited.

Nickel laterite ore

Naturally occurring heterogeneous nickel-bearing regolith formed by intense weathering of ultramafic parent rocks, including oxide-rich limonitic, clay-rich and hydrous magnesium-silicate-rich horizons. The USGS deposit model grounds this geological resource identity; excavated or stockpiled unprocessed ore remains within its scope. It is distinct from nickel metal, a mineral species such as goethite, a product-grade commodity and processing-generated laterite tailings. Individual ore samples differ in mineralogy and nickel, cobalt and chromium distribution; their laboratory leaching outcomes do not apply to every laterite or establish extraction from a pure host mineral.

Nickeline-bearing ore

Natural heterogeneous nickel-arsenide-bearing ore containing nickeline with carbonate and quartz gangue. The present evidence concerns a low-grade sample collected approximately 2.5 km northwest of Patang village in eastern Iran and a high-grade sample from Talmesi mine in central Iran. These are distinct feed arms within one geological resource class, not processing wastes, pure nickeline, nickel metal or a standardized concentrate grade. Boroumand et al.'s supplementary Table S1 reports nickel and arsenic contents of 4.93% and 5% for the low-grade feed, and 33.4% and 50% for the high-grade feed. Those are source-reported bulk sample compositions, not mineral formulas or general specifications. XRD and reflected-light observations identify nickeline with carbonate/quartz phases; whole-ore results cannot establish pure-phase selectivity. No criticality-list status is inferred from nickel.

Polymetallic nodules

Naturally occurring marine iron-manganese oxide and hydroxide concretions, commonly occurring on abyssal sediments and containing variable nickel, copper, cobalt and other constituents. This resource identity denotes the nodules, not a pure mineral species, constituent element, processing residue or defined traded metal grade. It is distinguished from rock-surface ferromanganese crusts and the terrestrial mixed manganese ore curated separately. Collection, drying and grinding do not make this geological feed a secondary resource. No constituent criticality designation is inherited.

Rare-earth-rich deep-sea mud

Naturally occurring deep-ocean sediment enriched in rare-earth elements and yttrium, commonly termed REY-rich mud. This record denotes the heterogeneous geological sediment resource, including pelagic clay with REY-bearing biogenic calcium phosphate grains documented in western North Pacific samples. It is distinct from individual rare-earth elements, a purified rare-earth commodity, an isolated apatite mineral, phosphorite, ferromanganese nodules and rock-surface ferromanganese crusts. Host phases and composition vary among deposits; no single mineral formula or universal grade defines this resource. Collection or physical concentration alone does not make the natural feed a secondary resource. No constituent criticality designation is inherited. A field-proxy study provides qualified evidence for an inferred microbial contribution to natural diagenetic iron transformations and REY redistribution; it does not demonstrate a controlled microbial extraction process, a recovered-metal yield, economic reserves or industrial deployment.

Seafloor massive sulfides

Naturally occurring polymetallic sulfide deposit material formed at or beneath the seafloor by hydrothermal mineralization. This resource includes heterogeneous sulfide-bearing ore and associated gangue, rather than a single mineral species, constituent element, finished metal commodity, or processing residue. USGS distinguishes this hydrothermal resource from polymetallic nodules and cobalt-rich ferromanganese crusts. The curated experiment used collected Japanese marine hydrothermal ores, not seawater alone or pure synthetic sulfides. Their compositions are sample-specific and do not define a universal resource grade. Collection, grinding and washing leave the feed a primary resource. No constituent criticality designation is inherited; the laboratory mechanism does not establish seafloor extraction or industrial deployment.

Natural silica-sand feed

Naturally occurring quartz-rich sand or disaggregated sandstone used as an industrial-mineral feed, represented here by two distinct English quarry samples. Yahaya's Silica 2 was a purple-red Oakamoor/Moneystone rock sample disaggregated to sand-grade powder; Silica 3 was yellow, uncemented Lower Greensand from Grandcourt near King's Lynn. The latter's XRD was dominated by quartz with minor phases assigned as kaolinite and feldspar. These are heterogeneous geological feeds, not the mineral species quartz, elemental silicon, spent foundry sand or a purity-defined silica product. The two samples' compositions and assay outcomes are not pooled. No constituent's criticality designation is transferred to this resource.

Tennantite concentrate

A mixed mineral concentrate enriched in source-labelled tennantite, represented here by the feed studied by Kondo et al. (2024). This resource is distinct from a pure mineral species, the separately described tennantite-(In), and elemental copper or arsenic. The study reports mineral liberation analysis of about 60 wt% tennantite, 23% pyrite, 8.2% sphalerite, 6.3% gangue, 1.6% galena and 0.97% chalcopyrite (rounded values). Bulk elemental composition is reported as S 31%, Cu 23%, Fe 12%, Zn 10%, Sb 7.8% and As 6.2%, with median particle size 41 micrometres. Its geographic origin and beneficiation route are not given. Generic discussion of flotation wastes does not establish that this particular feed was discarded waste. The informal tennantite phase label does not resolve a modern mineral species or establish the ideal Cu12As4S13 composition of every grain. No criticality designation is transferred from a constituent element.

Tetrahedrite concentrate

A mixed flotation concentrate enriched in source-labelled tetrahedrite, represented by the Strieborná vein feed from the Mária Mine in the Rožňava ore field, eastern Slovakia, studied by Kupka et al. (2025). This extracted geological feed is distinct from a modern tetrahedrite mineral species, elemental copper or antimony, and a waste stream. The source describes tetrahedrite as the principal sulfide, accompanied by siderite and quartz, minor pyrite, arsenopyrite and chalcopyrite, and trace other sulfides. Table 1 reports bulk Cu 25.59, Sb 19.68 and Ag 0.57 wt%. Its approximate phase-proportion prose is inconsistent with its bulk SiO2 value, so no reconciled quantitative mineral assemblage is assigned here. Modern tetrahedrite suffix species are distinct identities; the paper's generic phase label and selected microprobe analyses do not establish a species identity for this heterogeneous feed. No criticality designation is transferred from its constituent elements.

Xenotime concentrate

A mixed natural mineral concentrate represented by the laboratory feed studied by He et al. (2024), containing source-assigned xenotime, monazite, zircon, almandine and ilmenite. Its deposit, supplier and concentration history are not specified in the reviewed article and supplement. Particle counting and density conversion estimated about 82.0 wt% xenotime and 2.7 wt% monazite; these are sample-specific phase estimates, not measured product purity. The feed is distinct from the xenotime-(Y) mineral species, monazite species, elemental rare earths, a rare-earth commodity and a secondary waste resource. No criticality designation is inherited from its constituent elements.
