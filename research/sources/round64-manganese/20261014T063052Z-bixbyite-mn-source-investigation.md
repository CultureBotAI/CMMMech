## Selection and identity

Bixbyite-(Mn) adds a distinct Mn(III) mineral and an acidic bacterial mineral-formation experiment. The modern name matters: [CNMNC decision 21-H](https://doi.org/10.5194/ejm-34-1-2022), Newsletter 64 page 6, makes bixbyite a series name and distinguishes its Mn3+-dominant and Fe3+-dominant members. The definition includes an orthorhombic Mn2O3 polytype. [IMA September 2026](https://cnmnc.units.it/files/editor/IMA_Master_List_(2026-09).pdf) page 27 lists bixbyite-(Mn), Mn2O3, redefined by a 2021 special procedure. The special-procedure year is not a sequential IMA approval identifier. No mineral CURIE or criticality designation is invented.

The [Bohu et al. 2015 experiment](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2015.00734) describes only a **bixbyite-like Mn oxide**. The record preserves that qualification: association with the Mn-rich member does not retrospectively certify a pure natural mineral or determine its polytype. This is stronger than a name-only lead because the original has Raman and diffraction results, but it is weaker than a compositionally refined mineral identification.

## Phase-specific assay

T-G1 came from a Mn-rich layer 70 cm beneath a former uranium-leaching heap at Ronneburg, sampled in November 2011. Laboratory formation used liquid peptone/yeast extract/glucose medium with 0.1, 1 or 10 mM MnSO4, 10 mM MES at pH 5.5, 15°C, darkness and no shaking. The paper describes visible liquid-culture oxidation after months and no pH change after 90 days, but does not clearly map Figure 3 analyses to one loading, exact harvest age or vessel gas exchange.

Figure 3 shows aggregates and associated cells, qualitative Mn/O-rich EDS with other elements, tentative Raman matching and XRD compared against bixbyite reference lines. Raman bands at 337, 489 and 698 cm−1 differ from the cited analogue at 314, 481 and 698 cm−1. Carotenoid bands are also present. The proposed explanation of incorporated elements is not established by qualitative EDS. Additional nanoparticles were too small for further characterization. No refined structure, complete composition, quantitative phase purity or direct bulk Mn-valence result is supplied.

Preparation differs by analysis: air-dried centrifuged material for Mo-Kalpha XRD; particles directly from liquid culture for Raman; glutaraldehyde fixation, pH 7.4 PBS washing, ethanol dehydration and drying for SEM; and hydrated unpreserved material for CLSM. No matched uninoculated long-incubation XRD/Raman series or in situ phase series demonstrates exclusive microbial necessity or rules out preparation effects and secondary transformations. Figure 1's no-Mn culture is a substrate-omission comparator, not sterile Mn-containing medium.

## Oxidation evidence, controls and null results

The separate approximately eight-day microplate experiment used 100 µM MnSO4, pH 5.5 or 7.2 and three biological replicates. Figure 4 shows increasing LBB signal at pH 5.5, a larger transient signal at pH 7.2, and a low control curve. The caption says both pH control curves overlap, but their separate preparation is not sufficiently specified to treat this as a matched long-term phase-formation control. KMnO4-calibrated LBB measures oxidizing equivalents, not bixbyite concentration or recovery yield.

The original explicitly reports **no solid Mn-oxide particles at pH 7.2** despite positive oxidation and Mn(III)-trapping assays. This null is retained. Oxidation and precipitation are distinct outcomes. The proposed H2O2/catalase explanation is not a direct test of a unique crystallization pathway.

Figures 5–7 show proteinase-K-sensitive acidic Mn(III) trapping, no statistically significant acidic SOD effect in the reported comparison, acidic laccase-like activity/transcript association and greater stationary-phase superoxide at pH 7.2. These concern oxidation/protein assays; no mutant rescue, purified enzyme product or unique causal gene establishes production of the Figure 3 mineral. Figure 6's blue coloration is disclosed image processing, not a biological pigment.

Source recipes also conflict: growth-assay methods specify 100 µM MnSO4 and pH 5.5/7.2, whereas discussion mentions 500 µM and pH 5.5/6.8. The ABTS assay states 0.5 mM but its printed 300 µL of 15 mM substrate in a 3 mL mixture implies 1.5 mM. No quantitative rate or enzyme-normalized comparison is adopted. The mineral-production outcome lacks an isolated mass yield or industrial deployment. The source layer's earlier birnessite/todorokite assignment is not converted into a natural bixbyite occurrence.
