Lansfordite mineral

Lansfordite is the hydrated magnesium carbonate mineral Mg(CO3)(H2O)5, conventionally MgCO3·5H2O, listed as grandfathered with original-description year 1888 in the September 2026 IMA-CNMNC list. The year is not an approval identifier. It is distinct from nesquehonite, other magnesium carbonates, magnesium metal and mixed mineral resources. Evidence below separates abiotic laboratory synthesis/dehydration from one natural occurrence associated with cactus decay; no constituent criticality, identified microbial producer or industrial recovery is inferred.

Microbial & Biochemical Mechanisms (2)

Abiotic low-temperature crystallization and condition-dependent dehydration

other

Hill et al. crystallized synthetic lansfordite from magnesium bicarbonate solution and observed replacement of exposed crystals by nesquehonite needles while their external morphology persisted. This is a qualitative laboratory transformation, not a universal dehydration endpoint: a separate natural cactus-derived sample became X-ray amorphous on air exposure.

Experimental Context & Analytical Limits:
Hill et al. calcined chromatographic-grade anhydrous MgCO3 at 700 degrees C for one hour, cooled the resulting MgO, and slurried 20 g in one litre of doubly distilled water in a two-litre autoclave at 15 degrees C. High-purity CO2 pressure was maintained at 700 kPa for one hour; after pressure release, solution was filtered through a 0.65-micrometre membrane. Open-beaker storage at 15 degrees C produced lansfordite on vessel walls and nesquehonite at the liquid surface after several days. At 0 degrees C, lansfordite was the only reported crystalline product. Crystals stored below about 10 degrees C remained transparent; higher temperature or reduced pressure produced opaque surfaces and inward-growing needles. Humidity, exposure duration, replicate count and conversion yield were not specified for that qualitative dehydration observation.
Curated Evidence Points:
Support Source: hill1982
Preparation text pp.453-454 supports the two distinct crystallization temperatures and mixed product at 15 degrees C. Table I and single-crystal analysis establish synthetic lansfordite identity. Page 454 and Figure 3 identify the surface coating and inward-growing needles as nesquehonite by XRD and illustrate the pseudomorphous replacement. The source did not report a controlled comparison with microbial inoculation.
Partial Source: hill1982
The abstract emphasizes preparation below 10 degrees C, but methods also report mixed lansfordite/nesquehonite at 15 degrees C; below 10 is not treated as an absolute formation boundary. Figure 4 is a separate 10 degrees C/min thermal ramp on an 11.23 mg sample in static air, not room-temperature transformation kinetics. Abstract versus body end temperatures differ approximately (300 versus 350 degrees C dehydration; 560 versus about 570 degrees C decarbonation), so no exact universal thermal threshold is adopted.
Partial Source: garvie2003
Page 1882 and Figure 4 describe natural lansfordite becoming opaque and X-ray amorphous when exposed to room-temperature air, explicitly without nesquehonite reflections. This differs from Hill et al. synthetic nesquehonite replacement. Material provenance, moisture, pressure and exposure conditions are not matched, so neither result is discarded and no universal product, rate or explanation for the contrast is asserted.

Lansfordite associated with the microbial decay environment of a saguaro

biomineralization Microbial

Garvie reported lansfordite in one decaying saguaro cactus collected in winter 2001 in Arizona. Microbial breakdown was proposed to supply a moist, CO2-enriched mineral-forming environment, but the phase-specific biological cause was not experimentally isolated. The accepted claim is a field association with decay, not proof that a named organism produces lansfordite or that microbes are necessary.

Experimental Context & Analytical Limits:
The study sampled recently fallen, decaying and decayed Carnegiea gigantea across south and west-central Arizona. Lansfordite was found in a single decaying cactus retaining putrefying tissue, as glassy tabular to short-prismatic crystals and clusters up to about 1 mm, described as free of nesquehonite and monohydrocalcite in those samples. Methods used powder XRD, optical microscopy and SEM-EDS for mineral identification; Figure 4 shows optical images. The decay community was not taxonomically resolved for lansfordite and no taxon CURIE is assigned. There was no matched sterile cactus, isolate culture or quantified phase-specific production assay. Weather averages cited for January 2001 are not measured growth temperatures or pocket microchemistry; the general report of warm pockets reaching 50 degrees C is not a measured lansfordite synthesis temperature.
Curated Evidence Points:
Partial Source: garvie2003
Analytical methods p.1880, the lansfordite paragraph p.1882 and Figure 4 p.1883 support the winter field occurrence and air-sensitive crystals. The article describes the samples as monomineralic but supplies no lansfordite-specific raw diffraction plot, refined phase fraction or full detection limit. Its low-temperature, high-CO2/moisture formation explanation is explicitly inferential rather than a controlled demonstration.
Partial Source: garvie2003
Subsequent summer collections did not reveal lansfordite, but the number of searched cacti and matched environmental measurements are not given, preventing an occurrence-frequency or temperature-threshold estimate. Fungal hyphae shown on nesquehonite and glushinskite, and the isotope data for calcium carbonates in Table 1, are not evidence identifying a lansfordite-producing taxon or tracing its carbon. The study provides no industrial deployment, isolated magnesium recovery or lansfordite-specific carbon-sequestration rate.
Context_only Source: hill1982
The separate abiotic synthesis provides a counterexample to any claim that lansfordite intrinsically requires microbes. It does not refute the narrower occurrence in a microbial decay environment, and its chemical conditions are not used as measured field conditions.
