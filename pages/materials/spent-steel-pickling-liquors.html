Spent steel-pickling liquors secondary resource

Acidic metal-bearing process liquids remaining after removal of oxide scale from steel surfaces. The accepted experiment uses hydrochloric-acid liquor collected from a steel-processing plant's pickling bath, subsequently filtered and diluted with laboratory 9K medium. This liquid feed is distinct from steelmaking slag, mill scale, electroplating-bath effluents, treatment sludge and synthetic iron-salt solutions. It is represented as one process-resource identity, without separate owners for acid recipes or facilities. Evidence here concerns culture-associated iron redox transformation and sediment formation in the hydrochloric-liquor arm; it does not establish performance for every steel-pickling liquor, isolated metal recovery or criticality.

Microbial & Biochemical Mechanisms (1)

Conditional culture-associated Fe(II) oxidation in diluted pickling liquor

redox transformation Microbial

Associated Organisms: Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans (NCBITaxon:920) [TaxonMech]

A culture assigned by the authors to Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans was associated with declining dissolved Fe(II) in filtered hydrochloric steel-pickling liquor mixed with 9K medium. Iron oxidation and formation of iron-sulfate-containing sediments are the source interpretation. The measured percentage endpoint is Fe(II) oxidation, not recovered iron yield, and reagent-derived iron, sulfate and ammonium cannot be assigned exclusively to the waste feed. The assay does not establish a particular molecular pathway or biological necessity for precipitation.

Experimental Context & Analytical Limits:
Sections 2.1-2.3, printed p. 1837, describe serial cultivation and colony isolation from sediment near a polymerization ferric-chloride factory outlet in Tianjin. The reported isolation culture used 50 mL in 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks, 30 degrees C and 100 rpm; these are explicitly culture conditions, not separately established working volume or temperature for every waste-liquor assay. The actual plant liquor was filtered at 0.22 micrometres and mixed with 9K medium, with late-exponential-phase inoculum and Fe(II) monitoring by dichromate titration. Inoculum quantity and exact waste-assay working volume are not given. The authors state that the 9K comparison's ferrous content was matched to each mixed medium. Figure 1 also includes low-response CK curves, but the inspected methods do not define CK preparation, inoculation or sterility; it is not treated as a fully documented matched sterile control. Section 2.4 specifies three repeats for single-factor pH/iron/chloride experiments, without establishing independent biological replication for every waste-fraction or sediment-characterization arm.

Curated Evidence Points:
Support Source: qin2016
Section 3.1 on p. 1838 and Figure 1 on p. 1839 support substantial Fe(II) oxidation in the 20%, 40% and 60% waste-volume mixtures, with progressively delayed responses relative to inoculated 9K medium. The source reports near-complete oxidation for 20% after about 45 h onward, and 99.9% for 60% at day 17, compared with near-complete oxidation in its 9K comparison by day 4. These are source-reported Fe(II) conversion percentages in mixed media, not fractions of original waste iron isolated or recovered. The 60% curve's prolonged lag is retained despite the abstract's favorable tolerance wording.
Partial Source: qin2016
Section 3.1 reports no ferrous oxidation after inoculation of 70% and 80% waste mixtures; no corresponding time-course graph is provided. Section 2.3 instead describes a nominal 20/40/60-to-100% mixing sequence. Neither a demonstrated effective undiluted-waste treatment nor an unreported 100% outcome is inferred. Figure 2 and Table 1 support strong inhibition at the tested low-pH settings, but the narrative's pH 1.3 growth threshold is not a separately tested Table 1 level. Oxidation curves do not themselves directly measure viable-cell growth.
Partial Source: qin2016
Sections 2.1-2.2 print 0.5 g/L FeSO4·7H2O in the culture recipe and raw liquor Fe(II) 250 mg/L, yet section 3.1 assigns 7.13, 5.35 and 3.57 g/L Fe(II) to the 20%, 40% and 60% mixtures. These values are not reconciled by the printed recipe. The raw-liquor Cl 310 mg/L and pH 0 also do not establish a complete consistent acid/ion balance. Table 1 labels chloride-ion concentrations, whereas later text sometimes calls the 200-800 mg/L levels NaCl/KCl concentrations. Figures 3-4 show delayed oxidation relative to 9K despite the conclusion's no-inhibition wording. Table 1 is a single-factor design, so the abstract's combined pH 1.4, Fe(II) 0.04 mol/L and chloride 800 mg/L statement is not adopted as a jointly tested operating point. The 40% discussion says growth resumed after 84 h but reached its endpoint at 80 h, while Figure 1b approaches its plateau later; no precise reconciled kinetic estimate is asserted.
Partial Source: qin2016
Figures 5-8 on pp. 1840-1842 show SEM morphology, EDS, XRD and FTIR for sediments from the 60% waste/9K arm and comparison material. Section 3.3.1 calls the principal phase potassium jarosite while printing (NH4)Fe3(SO4)2(OH)6; the abstract and conclusion use the ammonium formula. This unresolved name/formula discrepancy and the absence of quantitative phase refinement prevent a phase-pure product claim. FTIR chloride interaction and adaptation are proposed interpretations, not demonstrated covalent binding or causal tolerance mechanisms. No solid-product mass balance, isolated iron yield, acid-reuse performance, cadmium fate, recovered-product purity or industrial treatment is established.
Context_only Source: genbankkf378735
The paper's KF378735 accession resolves to KF378735.1, a partial 16S sequence assigned to A. ferrooxidans strain XJF-8 with source qualifiers coal mine drainage and China: Shanxi. These differ from the Tianjin factory-outlet isolation in section 2.1. The species label is retained as the authors' assignment; the experimental culture is not equated with a verified XJF-8 deposit, type strain or whole genome, and sequence provenance does not prove function.
Context_only Source: ncbi920
Current NCBI taxonomy confirms the species label Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans and its historical Thiobacillus ferrooxidans synonym. This resolves nomenclature only, not the study's culture-provenance conflict or causal contribution relative to incompletely described controls.
